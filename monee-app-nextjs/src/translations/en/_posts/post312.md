**What to say:** send one short, friendly message that gives the amount and a date. For example: *"Hey, just a quick reminder about the $80 I covered. Can you send it back by Friday?"* Don't apologize and don't over-explain. If they can't pay by that date, ask them for a date they can manage. That one sentence does most of the work. The rest of this guide covers follow-ups, replies to common excuses, and your options if they never pay.

If a friend hasn't paid you back yet, bringing it up isn't petty. Asking won't ruin the friendship. Most people put this moment off because they don't want to seem rude, needy or dramatic. Being direct, calm and specific is what actually works.

## Before you send anything: get your facts straight

Check the exact amount, the date you paid or lent it, and what you agreed (if anything). A bank statement, payment-app history or old text thread is enough. A grounded opener like *"I was going through my spending and noticed the [amount] from [date] is still open"* sounds factual instead of emotional. Tracking also gives you confidence, because you're working from numbers, not frustration.

## Text scripts for the first ask

**Basic:**

> Hey, just a quick reminder about the [amount] I covered. Can you send it back by [date]?

**Softer:**

> Hey, I wanted to follow up on the [amount] from [event/date]. Could you send it back by [date]? Thanks.

**Firmer:**

> Hey, I need to settle the [amount] I lent you. Please send it by [date]. If that timing doesn't work, let me know today and we can agree on a plan.

All three work because they:
- name the issue and the amount clearly,
- give a deadline,
- leave room for a plan instead of a vague "soon."

## Using a payment-app request

If you both use a payment app, a request makes paying you a one-tap job. In the US, for example, Venmo lets you tap **Pay or Request**, choose the person, enter the amount, add a note and tap **Request**, and they can pay straight from the app ([Venmo](https://venmo.com/financial-education/how-to-send-receive-money)). Other apps have their own versions, and what's available depends on your country.

Send a quick personal message first, then the request. An unexplained request out of nowhere can feel cold. Example note for the request:

> For [event/date] — thanks!

## Email script

Keep the subject line plain:
- Repayment reminder
- Quick follow-up on the [amount]
- Checking in about repayment

> Hi [Name],
> I wanted to follow up on the [amount] I lent you on [date]. Please send it by [date]. If you can't do that, reply with a date that works so we can agree on a plan.
> Thanks,
> [Your name]

## What to say on a call or in person

Keep your opening line steady and brief:

> I wanted to bring up the [amount] I lent you. Can you repay it by [date]?

Then stop talking and let them answer. The silence may feel awkward, but it helps.

## How to respond to common replies

| They say | You say |
|---|---|
| "I forgot." | "Thanks for telling me. Can you send it by [date]?" |
| "I'm a bit tight right now." | "I understand. What date can you commit to? If you need to split it, let's set a plan now." |
| "I'll do it soon." | "Let's make it specific. What date should I expect it?" |
| "Why are you making this a big deal?" | "I'm not trying to make it a big deal. I just want to close the loop on money between us." |
| "I thought it was a gift." | "I can see how that got mixed up. I meant it as a loan. Can we sort out a date?" |

The "big deal" reply matters most. It keeps you out of a fight and brings the conversation back to the point.

If you want to protect the friendship and still be clear:

> I care about our friendship, which is exactly why I'd rather be direct than weird about it. Can we sort out the [amount] by [date]?

## Follow-up messages if they don't pay

Many people give up after one message because they feel embarrassed. Don't. Following up is normal.

**Second follow-up:**

> Following up again on the [amount]. I need us to settle this by [date]. Please confirm when you'll send it.

**Final direct message:**

> I've followed up a few times about the [amount] and haven't heard back. I need a reply by [date] with either payment or a repayment plan.

At this point you stop keeping it casual. Calm is still fine. Vague is not.

## Put any repayment plan in writing

If they agree to pay in parts, confirm it in a message:

> Thanks. To confirm: you'll send [amount] on [date] and the remaining [amount] by [date].

This protects you both from "I thought we said something else" later. It also gives you a written record if you ever need one.

## What if they never pay you back?

Then you have three realistic options:

1. **Let it go on purpose.** If the amount is small, decide it was a gift and stop chasing it. Your peace of mind may be worth more than the money. Just don't lend to that person again.
2. **Send a formal demand.** In England, Citizens Advice suggests a "letter before claim" as the first step toward court. It should state what happened, the amount and how you calculated it, a deadline to respond (usually 14 days), and that you'll start court proceedings if they don't reply ([Citizens Advice](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/law-and-courts/legal-system/taking-legal-action/small-claims/going-to-court/taking-court-action/step-one-write-a-letter-before-action)). In other countries, a written demand that names a deadline is also a common step before legal action.
3. **Use small claims court.** The rules and limits depend on where you live:
   - **England and Wales:** money claims under £10,000 generally go through the small claims process, and you can usually file online ([Citizens Advice](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/law-and-courts/legal-system/taking-legal-action/small-claims/making-a-small-claim/)).
   - **California (US):** individuals can claim up to $12,500 in small claims court ([Superior Court of California, County of San Francisco](https://www.sfsuperiorcourt.org/self-help/small-claims)). A 2026 bill to raise this to $15,000 (AB 1827) was held in committee and has not become law ([California Legislative Information](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billStatusClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260AB1827)).
   - **Other US states and countries:** limits, fees and time limits for filing vary, so check your local court's website before you file.

Going to court over a friend's debt usually ends the friendship. It makes the most sense when the amount is large and you have proof that it was a loan, such as texts, a transfer record or a written plan.

## What not to say

Don't load the message with emotional history. Avoid lines like "I guess I just care more than you do" or "This is why I never lend money." You may feel that way, but saying it won't get you paid back. Sort out the repayment first. Decide what the friendship means to you afterward.

## Next time: set terms before you lend

If this changes how you lend money, that's useful. You can be generous and still have boundaries:

> I'm happy to help. Let's be clear now about when you'll pay me back.

That isn't cold. It's just being clear, like an adult. It helps to name your limits early. Our guide on [what to say when a friend suggests an expensive plan](https://monee-app.com/blog/what-to-say-when-a-friend-suggests-an-expensive-plan/) uses the same approach. And if you're ever on the other side of this, see [what to say when you need more time to pay a bill](https://monee-app.com/blog/what-to-say-when-you-need-more-time-to-pay-a-bill/). It works for friends too.

The conversation you're avoiding is usually easier than the buildup in your head. Keep it short. Be specific. Ask for a date, and ask again if you need to. You don't need a big speech, just one steady sentence and the willingness to send it.
