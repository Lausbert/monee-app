**Short answer: yes, if you'll use it.** Using current US prices, a basic homemade loaf costs about **$1.00 to $1.20** in ingredients and electricity. A loaf of the same weight from the store costs about **$2.70**. A budget machine pays for itself in about a year if you bake once a week. A premium machine takes several years to pay off. So the real question isn't whether homemade bread is cheaper. It's whether you'll actually keep baking.

Here's how the numbers work out, so you can plug in your own.

## What does store-bought bread cost right now?

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks the average price of white pan bread. In August 2026 it was **$1.82 per pound** ([BLS via FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/APU0000702111)). Whole wheat costs more: **$2.81 per pound** in May 2026, the latest month on record ([Supermarket Perimeter / BLS data](https://www.supermarketperimeter.com/articles/13655-bureau-of-labor-statistics-resumes-reporting-on-bread-prices)).

Most bread machine recipes make a 1.5-pound loaf. So the store-bought version of that loaf costs:

- **White:** 1.5 lb × $1.82 ≈ **$2.73**
- **Whole wheat:** 1.5 lb × $2.81 ≈ **$4.22**

Keep in mind these are national averages. Your store brand might cost less, and bakery bread will cost a lot more.

## What does a homemade loaf cost?

Here's a **hypothetical** basic white loaf of 1.5 pounds, priced with current US data.

| Ingredient | Amount | Estimated cost |
|---|---|---|
| All-purpose flour | 3 cups (~360 g, ~0.79 lb) | ~$0.43 |
| Instant/bread machine yeast | ~2 tsp | ~$0.30 |
| Sugar, salt, oil or butter | Small amounts | ~$0.20–$0.40 (rough estimate) |
| Electricity | ~0.36 kWh | ~$0.07 |
| **Total** | | **~$1.00–$1.20** |

How these numbers were worked out:

- **Flour:** All-purpose flour averaged **$0.548 per pound** in August 2026 ([BLS via FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/APU0000701111)). King Arthur puts a cup of all-purpose flour at about 120 g ([King Arthur ingredient weight chart](https://www.kingarthurbaking.com/learn/ingredient-weight-chart)). Bread flour usually costs more, so expect your number to go up a little if you use it.
- **Yeast:** Walmart lists a 4 oz jar of Fleischmann's Bread Machine Yeast at **$5.48**, which equals 16 packets ([Walmart](https://www.walmart.com/ip/Fleischmann-s-Classic-Bread-Machine-Yeast-4-oz/10306744)). That's about 34¢ per packet, or roughly 30¢ for 2 teaspoons. Yeast is often the second-biggest cost, so buying it in bulk helps.
- **Electricity:** In tests reported by the Real Bread Campaign, Which? measured a standard white loaf at about **0.36 kWh** ([Sustain / Real Bread Campaign](https://www.sustainweb.org/realbread/bread_machines/)). The machine only heats for part of the cycle, so it uses far less than its wattage rating suggests. The average US home paid **18.34¢ per kWh** in June 2026 ([EIA data via Energy Factbook](https://energyfactbook.com/electric-power-monthly/)). That works out to about **7¢ per loaf**. Even in high-rate states it stays under 20¢.

**Bottom line:** a homemade loaf costs about **70–80¢ per pound**. Store-bought white bread costs about **$1.82 per pound**. That saves you roughly **$1.50–$1.70 per 1.5-lb loaf**.

## How many loaves until the machine pays for itself?

That depends on what you pay for the machine. Here are some current price points:

- **Used machines:** Used Hamilton Beach units on eBay start around **$35** ([eBay](https://www.ebay.com/b/Hamilton-Beach-2-lbs-Bread-Machines/20669/bn_7117794216)).
- **Budget new:** The Hamilton Beach Artisan 2 lb is listed at about **$85** at Lowe's ([Lowe's](https://www.lowes.com/pd/Hamilton-Beach-Hamilton-Beach-Red-and-Stainless-Steel-2-lb-Artisan-Dough-and-Bread-Maker/5001925157)).
- **Mid-range:** Cuisinart models run roughly **$120–$255**, depending on the model ([The Consumers Guide](https://www.theconsumers.guide/reviews/best-bread-machines-home-baking-2026)).
- **Premium:** The Zojirushi Virtuoso Plus tops many review lists. Tom's Guide says it costs nearly **$400** ([Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/uk/best-picks/best-bread-machines)).

Assuming you save about $1.60 per white loaf, here's the break-even point:

| Machine price | Loaves to break even | At 1 loaf/week | At 2 loaves/week |
|---|---|---|---|
| $35 (used) | ~22 | ~5 months | ~3 months |
| $85 (budget) | ~53 | ~1 year | ~6 months |
| $150 (mid-range) | ~94 | ~1.8 years | ~11 months |
| $400 (premium) | ~250 | ~4.8 years | ~2.4 years |

If you normally buy whole wheat or bakery bread, you'll save more per loaf and break even sooner. If you usually buy the cheapest store-brand loaf, you'll save less and it'll take longer.

## What the math doesn't show

**Is your time worth something?** Measuring takes about five minutes, but the cycle runs for hours. Which? timed a standard white loaf at about 3 hours 18 minutes ([Real Bread Campaign](https://www.sustainweb.org/realbread/bread_machines/)). Delay timers help a lot here.

**Will you actually eat it before it goes stale?** Homemade bread has no preservatives, so it goes stale faster. If you throw out half a loaf, you lose most of what you saved. Slicing it and freezing it fixes that.

**Do you have counter space?** A machine you have to dig out of a cupboard tends to stop getting used. A machine sitting in a cupboard never pays for itself.

**Could you just use your oven?** Yes, and you'd skip the machine cost completely. But a UK Energy Saving Trust estimate from 2008 put an electric oven at about 1.6 kWh per bake, compared with roughly 0.36 kWh for a bread machine ([Real Bread Campaign](https://www.sustainweb.org/realbread/bread_machines/)). You'd also be kneading by hand.

**What about extras?** Replacement paddles or pans and specialty flours all add to the cost. Fancy mix-ins like seeds, nuts, or cheese can push a loaf's cost close to the store price.

## So, should you buy one?

**A bread maker probably makes sense if:**
- Your household eats at least one loaf a week
- You often buy whole wheat, specialty, or bakery bread
- You like knowing what's in your food (no preservatives, less sugar or salt)
- You're fine with starting at a budget or used machine

**You might want to skip it if:**
- You eat bread only now and then
- You mostly buy the cheapest store loaf, so you'd save less
- You're short on counter space
- You're tempted by a $400 model but aren't sure you'll stick with it

A good rule of thumb: start cheap. A used or budget machine lets you find out whether baking at home fits your routine. If it does, it pays for itself fast. If it doesn't, you haven't lost much.
