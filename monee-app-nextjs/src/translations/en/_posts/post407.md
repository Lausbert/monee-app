**Short answer:** In the United States, USDA's September 2026 food plans work out to about **$2.80 per person per meal** on the lowest-cost (Thrifty) plan and about **$4.60** on the most generous (Liberal) plan. These figures cover food cooked at home, and the per-meal amounts include snacks. The right target for you is what your household can keep spending without strain: take your monthly grocery budget, set aside money for snacks and staples, and divide what's left by the servings you plan to cook.

The sections below give the benchmarks first, then show how to build your own target and work out a recipe's cost per serving.

## How much does a meal cost at home? 2026 US benchmarks

The USDA publishes monthly cost estimates for four food plans: Thrifty, Low-Cost, Moderate-Cost and Liberal. The latest reports cover September 2026 ([USDA Food Plans: Monthly Cost of Food Reports](https://www.fns.usda.gov/research/cnpp/usda-food-plans/cost-food-monthly-reports)). For the reference family of two adults aged 20–50 and two children aged 6–8 and 9–11, the Thrifty plan is about **$1,024 per month**, up 2.18% from September 2025 ([American Default Research, compiling USDA data](https://americandefault.org/affordability/food-budgets/)). Secondary summaries of the same September 2026 report give these monthly amounts for the other plans ([NumYum summary of USDA data](https://www.numyum.ai/blog/usda-thrifty-food-plan-2026)):

| USDA plan (US, Sept 2026) | Family of four per month | Per person per month | Approx. per person per meal* |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Thrifty | ~$1,024 | ~$256 | ~$2.80 |
| Low-Cost | ~$1,127 | ~$282 | ~$3.10 |
| Moderate-Cost | ~$1,389 | ~$347 | ~$3.80 |
| Liberal | ~$1,682 | ~$420 | ~$4.60 |

*This is our own rough calculation: the per-person monthly cost divided by about 91 meals (3 meals × 30.4 days). Snacks are spread across the meals.

How to read these numbers:

- **They are benchmarks, not rules.** The plans assume all meals and snacks are made at home, and USDA updates them every month for food price inflation.
- **The household includes children.** Children eat less than adults, so a single adult's per-meal cost usually sits above these per-person averages. USDA also adjusts the figures for household size. Smaller households cost more per person, and a person living alone gets about 20% added.
- **They are US national averages.** Alaska and Hawaii have their own Thrifty figures, and prices in your local area may be higher or lower. If you live outside the US, use your own country's household budget or food cost statistics.

## Average food budget: what US households actually spend

Recommended budgets and actual spending are different things. According to the US Bureau of Labor Statistics, the average US consumer unit (roughly a household) spent **$6,491 on food at home in 2025**, or about $541 a month. It spent another **$4,142 on food away from home**, about $345 a month. That makes total food spending $10,633, or 13.0% of all spending ([BLS Consumer Expenditures—2025, released October 29, 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cesan_10292026.htm)). BLS notes that the 2025 survey missed data for October and November 2025 during the federal funding lapse and adjusted its weights to make up for it.

These averages mix together households of every size, income and diet. Use them as context, not as your target.

## The basic cost-per-meal formula

For a quick average, use:

**Monthly grocery budget ÷ planned meal servings = average cost per serving**

Count servings, not recipes or cooking sessions. A dinner for four counts as four servings, even if everyone eats together.

As a hypothetical example, take a two-person household with a monthly grocery budget of $600 that expects to make 144 servings:

**$600 ÷ 144 = $4.17 per serving**

This is a useful starting point, but it treats every purchase and every meal the same. A more practical target also accounts for snacks, drinks and pantry supplies, and for the fact that dinner often costs more than breakfast. If you prefer to think in daily amounts, see [how to set a daily spend target with budget ÷ days](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-set-a-daily-spend-target-with-budget-days/).

## Start with a realistic grocery budget

Look over two or three months of grocery spending before you pick a target. Include:

- Food and nonalcoholic drinks eaten at home
- Grocery delivery charges and tips, if you pay them
- Ingredients bought in bulk
- Food you bought but ended up throwing away

Keep restaurant meals, takeout and delivery in a separate dining-out category. Otherwise one expensive order can throw off the target you use for home cooking. If you're deciding between cooking, meal kits and takeout, a [weighted cost-time matrix](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-decide-between-meal-kits-takeout-and-home-cooking-with-a-weighted-costtime-matrix/) helps you compare them fairly.

If you want an outside check, the USDA plans above are the standard US benchmark. Your own affordable budget should still come first.

## Set aside money that doesn't belong to a meal

Not every grocery item fits neatly into breakfast, lunch or dinner. Before you work out meal targets, set aside part of the budget for things like:

- Snacks
- Coffee, tea and other drinks
- Condiments and cooking oils
- Spices and baking supplies
- Some unavoidable food waste

Setting aside 5% to 15% is a reasonable first guess. The right share depends on what your household buys, so check your receipts rather than treating that range as a rule.

In the hypothetical $600 budget, a 10% reserve puts $60 toward these items and leaves $540 for planned meals.

## Give each meal its own target

A single average can make a simple breakfast look unusually cheap and a balanced dinner look too expensive. Split the meal budget to match how your household eats.

Here is one hypothetical split for two people:

| Meal | Monthly servings | Share of $540 | Monthly amount | Target per serving |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Breakfast | 52 | 25% | $135 | $2.60 |
| Lunch | 44 | 30% | $162 | $3.68 |
| Dinner | 48 | 45% | $243 | $5.06 |

These figures are examples, not recommended prices. Someone who buys lunch at work or skips breakfast would use different serving counts and shares.

Your targets should also reflect dietary needs, appetite, local prices and how much time you have to cook. A lower number isn't automatically better if it leads to meals that leave people hungry or that keep getting replaced with takeout.

## How much should lunch cost per day?

There's no official figure for lunch. Work it out from your own budget instead:

- **Packed lunch:** use your lunch target from the table above. In the hypothetical example that's $3.68 per serving, which sits close to the USDA per-meal benchmarks.
- **Bought lunch:** this belongs in your dining-out budget, not your grocery budget. Decide how many bought lunches you can afford each month and divide that money by the number. If you only buy lunch on office days, a [cost-per-office-day cap](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-budget-for-returntooffice-days-with-a-costperofficeday-cap/) keeps commuting, coffee and lunch costs together under one daily limit.

## Calculate a recipe's cost per serving

For each recipe, add up the cost of the amounts you actually use:

**Cost of ingredients used ÷ edible servings produced = cost per serving**

Suppose a hypothetical pot of soup uses:

- $4.50 of vegetables
- $2.00 of beans
- $1.25 of stock
- $1.00 of oil, herbs and seasoning

The recipe costs $8.75. If it makes five servings:

**$8.75 ÷ 5 = $1.75 per serving**

Don't charge a whole package to one recipe if you'll use the rest later. If a $6 bottle of oil holds 48 tablespoons and the recipe uses two, the oil adds $0.25.

To compare the price per serving of packaged foods, divide the package price by the number of servings you'll actually eat, which may differ from the label. Shelf unit prices (per ounce or per pound) help you compare package sizes.

Small amounts of salt or spices don't need perfect precision. A small pantry allowance is easier to keep up than tracking every fraction of a cent. The same method shows whether an appliance pays for itself. For examples, see the [slow cooker cost-per-meal test](https://monee-app.com/blog/should-you-buy-a-slow-cooker-a-cost-per-meal-test/).

## Account for leftovers and waste honestly

A serving only counts if someone is likely to eat it. A $12 casserole isn't really $2 per meal if three of its six portions get thrown out.

Plan leftovers for a specific lunch, freezer meal or reworked dish. USDA MyPlate also recommends checking the food you already have, planning meals for the week and using leftovers on purpose to cut waste ([MyPlate: Make a Plan](https://www.myplate.gov/eathealthy/budget/budget-weekly-meals)).

When tracking costs:

- Count leftovers when they're eaten or stored for a planned meal.
- Spread bulk purchases across the recipes that use them.
- Record regular waste so future meal plans use more realistic quantities.
- Compare unit prices only when you can use up the larger package before it spoils.

## Treat the target as a guide, not a hard limit

Meal costs will vary from day to day. One dinner may go over its target while a breakfast comes in well under. What matters is whether the weekly or monthly average stays within budget.

After four weeks, review the results:

1. Work out the actual cost per serving for several common meals.
2. Compare total grocery spending with the monthly budget.
3. Look for waste, unplanned purchases or unrealistic serving estimates.
4. Adjust the meal targets instead of forcing every recipe under the same limit.

Check the numbers again when household size, dietary needs, income or local prices change. In the US, the USDA food plans change every month, so compare against the latest report. The best cost-per-meal target protects the rest of your budget while still covering meals your household will actually cook, eat and enjoy.
