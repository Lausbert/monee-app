Here's the short answer. Set one total gift budget as a couple first. Then pick a fairness rule you both agree on: equal per family, equal per person, or based on each family's traditions. Divide the money into tiers before you buy anything. Fair doesn't always mean identical. It means you both agreed on the rule before the shopping started.

Money is on a lot of people's minds this season. In the US, the National Retail Federation's 2026 holiday survey found that [56% of shoppers expect economic conditions to affect their holiday spending](https://nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/affordability-shapes-winter-holiday-shopping-according-to-nrf-holiday-survey). A clear plan for two families helps you keep the total under control and keeps arguments out of December.

## Step 1: Agree on one total before you split anything

Think of it like cooking for a big dinner. You decide how much food to make before you start filling plates. If each partner sets a budget for their own family alone, the total grows quietly and nobody owns it.

Sit down together and answer three questions:

1. **What can we spend on gifts this season without using debt?** Start from your actual monthly budget, not a number you hope for.
2. **What else is coming out of the holiday pot?** Travel, hosting food, decorations, and cards often come from the same money. Decide whether they count toward the gift total or have their own line.
3. **Who outside the families is on the list?** Teachers, coworkers, friends, and hosts all add up. If kids are in school, check [how much to spend on a teacher gift](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-much-should-you-spend-on-a-teacher-gift/) and set that money aside first.

Whatever is left after the non-family gifts is the amount you split between the two families.

## Step 2: Choose a fairness rule

Most couples use one of these four methods. None of them is the right one for everybody. The best rule is the one you both can explain to your relatives without getting defensive.

1. **50/50 by family.** Each side gets the same amount, whatever its size. It's simple and easy to defend, but it can feel thin if one family is twice as big.
2. **Equal per person.** Every recipient gets roughly the same amount, so the bigger family gets more money in total. Each person is treated the same, though the totals won't match.
3. **Tiered by closeness.** Parents get the most, then siblings, then nieces and nephews, then extended family. Both sides use the same tier amounts.
4. **Tradition-based.** One family does big gift piles while the other does a single gift exchange. You budget for each family's actual custom instead of forcing a match.

Many couples end up mixing these, for example equal tiers but tradition-based for extended relatives. That's fine, as long as you choose the mix on purpose.

## Step 3: Build the tiers (a hypothetical example)

*This example is hypothetical and only meant to show the math.*

Say a couple has $1,000 for family gifts. Partner A's family has 2 parents, 1 sibling, and 2 young kids. Partner B's family has 2 parents, 3 siblings, a sibling's spouse, and 1 kid.

Using **equal tiers**:

| Tier | Amount per person | Family A | Family B |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parents | $100 | $200 | $200 |
| Siblings and spouses | $50 | $50 | $200 |
| Kids | $40 | $80 | $40 |
| **Total** | | **$330** | **$440** |

That comes to $770. The other $230 is a buffer for shipping, wrapping, a host gift if you travel, and the price surprises that always show up. If you're staying with either family, a small thank-you is normal. Use the guide to [what to spend on a dinner party host gift](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-much-should-you-spend-on-a-dinner-party-host-gift/) for a rough range.

Family B gets more money here because it has more people. Under this rule, that's fair. If 50/50 matters more to you both, lower Family B's sibling tier until the totals match.

## Step 4: Handle the tricky parts

**One family spends a lot more.** You don't have to match their spending. Gifts aren't a scoreboard. Give what fits your budget. If they ask, a warm, plain answer works: "We're keeping things simpler this year."

**Unequal incomes or separate finances.** If you each pay for your own family's gifts, agree on a range anyway. Otherwise one partner's family can end up looking clearly favored, and that tends to come up eventually.

**Travel costs.** If you spend $600 on flights to see one side, some couples count the visit itself as part of that family's "gift." Talk this through openly. Don't quietly cut that family's gift budget.

**Big families.** Suggest a Secret Santa or a name draw with a set price. Like a sports team rotating positions, everyone takes part but no one carries the whole load.

**Kids get the spotlight.** Many families care more about gifts for children than for adults. Putting more money into the kids' tier and less into the adults' tier often goes down well on both sides.

## Step 5: Track it as you go

A budget only works if you check it while you shop, not in January. Keep a simple list with each name, the planned amount, and the actual spend. Use a shared note, a spreadsheet, or a budgeting app where you both log purchases. Tag each purchase "Family A" or "Family B" so you can see at a glance if one side is running over.

When you overspend on one gift, take it from the buffer or from the same tier. Don't take it from the other family. That keeps your fairness rule in place.

## Make next year easier

The holidays aren't the only time both families expect gifts. Birthdays, anniversaries, and new babies come up all year. Setting up a [yearly gift fund](https://monee-app.com/blog/birthday-budget-set-a-yearly-gift-fund-without-guessing/) means December doesn't have to carry the whole cost. Saving a little each month for the holidays works the same way as meal prep: an hour of planning saves a week of scrambling. Some shoppers already spread the cost out. In NRF's 2026 survey, half of US shoppers who start before November said [they do it to spread out their budgets](https://nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/affordability-shapes-winter-holiday-shopping-according-to-nrf-holiday-survey).

After the season, spend ten minutes going over what happened. Note which gifts landed well, where you overspent, and whether the split felt fair to you both. Change the tiers for next year and write the rule down.
