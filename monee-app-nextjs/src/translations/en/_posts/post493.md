To stop duplicate gifts, the whole family needs **one shared list where givers can claim an item before they buy it.** The person getting the gifts shouldn't be able to see those claims. Everyone else should.

A list on its own won't stop duplicates. The claiming step does that. The same idea works when several families are buying for one teacher: either use a shared sign-up where each family claims an item, or pool the money into one class gift.

This guide covers how to set up the list, how to keep the surprise, how to include relatives who won't use an app, and how to handle teacher gifts.

## Why duplicate gifts happen

Duplicate gifts usually come from good intentions. Two aunts hear the same hint, or a grandparent and a parent both spot the same toy on sale.

Nobody did anything wrong. They just couldn't see what the others were planning. A family wish list gives everyone that view without spoiling things for the person opening the gifts.

## Step 1: Pick one place for the list

Which tool you use matters less than everyone using the same one. If the family keeps two lists in two places, duplicates come right back.

Common options:

- **A dedicated wish list app.** On Giftster, by default, the list maker [can't see which items are reserved or purchased, or who marked them](https://help.giftster.com/article/72-will-list-maker-see-reserved-purchased), but other shoppers can. Elfster works in a similar way. Other viewers see that a wish has been bought, but the [Wishlist owner isn't notified and can't see who bought it](https://help.elfster.com/en/articles/11517983-how-do-i-mark-that-i-bought-an-item-on-another-elfster-wishlist).
- **A retailer's gift list.** On Amazon (amazon.com, US), a Gift List has a ["Keep it a Surprise" setting](https://www.amazon.com/gp/help/customer/display.html?nodeId=G85CQ7LT2BVSPWWE). Turning off "Show what's been purchased" hides bought items from the owner. People shopping from the list can always see what has been bought.
- **A shared spreadsheet or document.** It's free and flexible. The catch is that you have to keep the recipient off the shared copy yourself.

If your family shops at many different stores, a store-neutral app or a spreadsheet is usually easiest. Giftster says it [doesn't get involved in the actual purchase](https://help.giftster.com/article/95-how-to-shop-this-list) and only manages the list, so you can buy the item anywhere you like.

## Step 2: Ask each person to write a useful list

A vague list leads to guessing, and guessing leads to doubles.

Ask everyone to include:

- **Specifics:** size, colour, model, or a link.
- **A rough price range**, so givers can pick something that fits their budget.
- **More items than they need.** A few extra ideas mean nobody gets stuck.
- **"Already have" notes** where they help. For example: "Have the first two books in this series."

You can also add a few non-physical ideas, like experiences or a contribution toward something bigger. If cost is a sensitive subject in your family, here's how to [ask relatives to keep gifts budget-friendly](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-ask-relatives-to-keep-gifts-budget-friendly/) without it feeling awkward.

## Step 3: Make the claiming rule clear

This is the step that actually stops duplicates. Agree on one simple rule:

> **Claim it before you buy it. Mark it purchased right after.**

In Giftster, shoppers can mark an item reserved before buying. Once every unit of an item has been claimed, Giftster [removes the reserve and purchase buttons and shows the item in a different colour](https://help.giftster.com/article/95-how-to-shop-this-list), so others can see it's taken. It also keeps a ["My Shopping List" of everything you've reserved or bought](https://help.giftster.com/article/99-how-to-use-my-shopping-list).

Encourage people to claim early, even if they won't buy for a while. A claim costs nothing, and it stops someone else from picking the same idea. If you're not sure when to actually buy, this [budget timing test for buying gifts early or waiting](https://monee-app.com/blog/should-you-buy-gifts-early-or-wait-a-budget-timing-test/) can help.

**A caution about store gift lists.** These usually track only orders placed through the list itself. If you buy the item elsewhere, or in a shop, go back and mark it yourself so the list stays accurate.

## Step 4: Protect the surprise

Givers should see the claims. The recipient shouldn't.

A few habits help:

- **Check the visibility settings** before you share the list. Defaults vary between tools and list types. For example, Giftster's Essentials lists [let the list maker choose whether to see purchases](https://www.giftster.com/essentials/).
- **Watch out for kids' lists.** On Giftster, a parent who manages a child account [can see reserves, purchases and the names of the shoppers](https://www.giftster.com/childrens-lists/) on that child's lists. That's handy for parents. Just make sure the child isn't looking at the parent's screen.
- **Keep spreadsheets private.** Make a separate "shopping copy" and don't invite the recipient to it.

## Step 5: Include relatives who don't use apps

Some family members won't download anything, and that's fine.

Choose one person to act as the go-between. That person calls or texts the non-app relatives and claims items for them.

Some tools also let guests mark items without signing up. On Giftster, guests viewing a public list [can mark items reserved or purchased without becoming members](https://help.giftster.com/article/73-how-to-shop-for-others). Even so, a single point person is often simpler.

## Step 6: Handle shared and group gifts

Bigger items sometimes get split between several people. To keep it tidy:

- Add a note on the item, like "Group gift: Mum, Sam and Lee."
- Have one person claim it for the whole group.
- Settle up soon after buying, so nobody quietly covers too much.

Splitting money between relatives can get tricky. A [simple budget-first rule for lending money to family](https://monee-app.com/blog/lending-money-to-family-a-simple-budget-first-rule/) can help you think it through.

## How to prevent duplicate teacher gifts from different families

Yes, you can stop several families from buying the same teacher gift. It works on the same principle as the family list: everyone needs to see what's already taken. There are two common approaches.

**1. A shared sign-up where each family claims an item.** The room parent or class rep lists specific items and sets a quantity for each, such as "4 boxes of tissues" rather than an open request. Families then claim slots. SignUpGenius, a US-based sign-up tool, says its sign-ups [update in real time when a parent claims an item](https://www.signupgenius.com/resources/teacher-wish-list), so the next family sees it's covered. It also says parents can claim items without creating an account. A plain shared spreadsheet does the same job if someone keeps it up to date.

**2. One pooled class gift.** If everyone chips in toward a single gift, such as a gift card the teacher chooses, duplicates can't happen. Tools such as [SignUpGenius Group Gifts](https://www.signupgenius.com/resources/group-gifts-for-teachers) collect contributions through one link and let the organiser send a digital gift card. Check the provider's own pricing page for current fees and supported countries before you start.

A few tips that keep a class gift fair:

- **Ask early and keep it voluntary.** Suggest a range rather than a fixed amount.
- **Sign it from the whole class**, not just the families who paid.
- **Check the school's gift policy first.** Rules differ by school, district and country, and some public schools have legal limits. In Massachusetts (US), for example, the State Ethics Commission says a public school teacher may accept [class gifts worth up to $150 in total over the school year](https://www.mass.gov/service-details/gifts-to-public-school-teachers-and-staff), as long as the gift is identified only as being from the class. Individual gifts must be worth less than $50 and can only come from families who didn't contribute to the class gift. The Commission's [Advisory 19-1](https://www.mass.gov/advisory/19-1-gifts-and-gratuities) has the details.

## A quick hypothetical example

*This is an invented example, not a real case.* Imagine a family of eight shopping for two kids. Everyone uses one shared app. Each child's list has about ten items, and each item has a price range.

Grandma claims a board game in October. Two weeks later, an uncle opens the list, sees the game is taken, and picks a book set instead.

Nobody had to go back and forth in the group chat, and nothing had to be returned afterwards. It worked because claiming came before buying.

## Small habits that keep it working

- **Update the list as wishes change.** Remove anything the person has already bought for themselves.
- **Set one "list ready by" date**, so early shoppers aren't choosing from a half-finished list.
- **Reuse the list for birthdays.** The same setup works all year.
- **Be gentle with slip-ups.** Someone will forget to mark a purchase at some point. A friendly reminder works better than a lecture about the rules.

## The short version

Use one shared list with clear, specific wishes. Claim an item before you buy it, mark it purchased right after, and keep the claims hidden from the person receiving the gifts. For teacher gifts, use a claim-based sign-up or one pooled class gift, and check the school's gift rules first.
