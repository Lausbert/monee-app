Here's the short answer. For most dinner parties in the US, **$15 to $30 is a comfortable range for a host gift.** For a very casual dinner with close friends, $10 or nothing at all is fine. For a first visit, a holiday party, or a special celebration, you might go up to $40 or $50 if your budget allows.

These numbers are a common rule of thumb. No official standard exists. The main etiquette authorities say what matters is the thought, not the price.

If you live outside the US, adjust the range to local prices. A small, nice item from your local market is the goal.

## Do you even have to bring a host gift?

You might feel pressure to show up with something impressive. You can let some of that go.

The Emily Post Institute says that ["guests don't have to bring a hosting gift to dinner,"](https://emilypost.substack.com/p/etiquette-today-all-about-hosting) and that casual dinners especially don't call for one. Close friends who host each other often tend to skip gifts or save them for special occasions.

The Institute does [recommend bringing a small gift the first time you visit someone's home](https://emilypost.com/advice/should-i-bring-a-hostess-gift). It also says that at large, formal dinners, gifts usually aren't brought, especially if you don't know the host well.

On spending, its advice is simple: ["your gift should always be within your means. No one has to break their budget for a hosting gift."](https://emilypost.substack.com/p/etiquette-today-all-about-hosting)

So if money is tight this month, you're not being rude by keeping it small.

## A simple spending guide by occasion

These ranges are general guidelines in US dollars. Use them as a starting point, not a rule.

| Occasion | Suggested range (US) | Good fits |
|---|---|---|
| Casual dinner with close friends | $0–$15 | A dessert, a bag of good coffee, offering to help clean up |
| Typical dinner party | $15–$30 | A bottle of wine, chocolates, a small plant |
| First visit to someone's home | $20–$35 | Something for the home, like nice napkins or a candle |
| Holiday or special celebration | $25–$50 | A nicer bottle, a gourmet food item, a small serving dish |
| Dinner hosted by your boss or a new acquaintance | $20–$40 | Something classic and neutral, like quality chocolates or olive oil |

You'll notice the ranges overlap. That's on purpose. Your relationship with the host matters more than the exact category.

## What does a bottle of wine actually cost?

Wine is the classic host gift, and it can be very affordable.

In the US, the average price of red and white table wine was [about $14.05 per liter in October 2026](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/APU0000720311), according to Bureau of Labor Statistics data published by the St. Louis Fed. That works out to roughly $10.50 for a standard 750 ml bottle. Keep in mind that this average covers all sizes and types, including boxed wine, so a gift-worthy bottle will usually cost a bit more.

In practice, a $15 to $25 bottle from a shop with helpful staff is a lovely gift. You can simply ask for "something nice to bring to a dinner party in this price range." Store staff get this request all the time.

One etiquette point: don't expect your host to open it that night. The Emily Post Institute says that with consumables like wine and chocolates, ["don't expect your host to serve them; it's up to the host to decide."](https://emilypost.com/advice/should-i-bring-a-hostess-gift) You can make it easy by saying, "This is for you to enjoy later."

## Gift ideas at every budget

You don't need to spend much to give something your host will remember.

**Under $15**
- A jar of local honey or jam
- A box of nice tea or a bag of coffee beans
- Homemade cookies or bread
- A small bunch of flowers, already in a jar so your host doesn't have to find a vase

**$15 to $30**
- A bottle of wine or sparkling cider
- A box of quality chocolates
- A small potted herb or houseplant
- A set of nice cocktail napkins

**$30 to $50**
- A good bottle of olive oil or a specialty food item
- A small serving bowl, cheese board, or platter
- A scented candle from a brand your host likes
- A nicer bottle of wine or Champagne

The Emily Post Institute suggests gifts that are ["small and easy for the host to receive while managing the greeting of guests."](https://emilypost.substack.com/p/etiquette-today-all-about-hosting) So skip anything that needs arranging, cooking, or a quick trip to the freezer when your host is busy at the door.

## When it makes sense to spend a little more

Sometimes going toward the top of your range feels right. That's okay, as long as it fits your budget.

You might choose to spend more when:

- **The host went to big lengths.** For a multi-course meal or a holiday feast, a slightly nicer gift says thank you.
- **It's a milestone.** If the dinner celebrates a promotion, an engagement, or a new home, the gift may double as a congratulations. For new-home dinners, the guide to [what to spend on a housewarming gift](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-much-should-you-spend-on-a-housewarming-gift/) may help.
- **You're a guest of honor.** If the dinner is for you, a gift or flowers sent ahead, plus a handwritten thank-you note, is a gracious touch.
- **You're staying overnight.** A weekend visit usually calls for a bit more than a single evening.

## When it's fine to spend less (or nothing)

You might choose to spend less when:

- **It's a potluck.** The dish you bring often counts as your contribution.
- **You eat together all the time.** Swapping dinners with close friends doesn't need a gift each time.
- **You're helping host.** If you're sharing the work or the costs, you're already giving. This guide to [splitting party costs fairly when co-hosting](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-split-party-costs-fairly-when-co-hosting/) covers that situation.
- **Money is tight.** A warm thank-you text the next day means a lot, and it costs nothing.

## Small ways to make a modest gift feel special

A thoughtful gift doesn't need a big price tag. You can make a small one feel personal by:

- **Choosing something your host loves.** A favorite snack or a local coffee shows you pay attention.
- **Adding a short note.** A few handwritten words make even a $10 gift feel warm.
- **Wrapping it simply.** A ribbon or a paper bag with tissue looks lovely.
- **Following up.** A thank-you message the next day is often remembered longer than the gift.

## Planning ahead for a busy social season

Host gifts can add up fast, especially from November through New Year's, when invitations pile up.

If you know a few dinners are coming, it can help to set a small monthly amount for gifts. Some people keep one "gifts" category in their budget that covers dinners, birthdays, and school events. If that sounds like you, you might also find the guides on [teacher gifts](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-much-should-you-spend-on-a-teacher-gift/) helpful for planning the rest of the season.

Buying a few small items when they're on sale also helps. A couple of nice jams or candles in a cupboard means you're never caught empty-handed or paying full price at the last minute.

## The bottom line

In the US, a dinner party host gift of about $15 to $30 is plenty for most occasions. Spend less for casual dinners with close friends, and a bit more for special celebrations if you can comfortably afford it.

Your host invited you for your company, not your gift. Showing up on time, being kind, and saying thank you afterward count more than what you spend.
