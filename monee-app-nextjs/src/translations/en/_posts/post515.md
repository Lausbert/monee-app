The short answer is **collect first, buy second.** Agree on a price, get everyone's money in by a deadline, and only buy the gift once the money is in your account. Nobody gets stuck carrying a $300 balance and quietly hoping Kevin from accounting remembers to pay.

That sounds simple. It's also the step most organizers skip. Someone finds the perfect gift, gets excited, buys it, and spends three weeks sending "just a friendly reminder 😊" messages. Here's how to skip that part.

## Step 1: Pick a price range before you pick a gift

Start with the budget and choose the gift after. If you fall in love with a $250 espresso machine before anyone has agreed to chip in, you've set yourself up to cover the gap.

Ask the group a simple question: "Are we thinking $10, $20, or $30 each?" Then multiply by the number of people you *know* will join, not the number you hope will join.

If you're not sure what's normal for the occasion, these guides can help you set a sensible range:

- [How much to spend on a baby shower gift](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-much-should-you-spend-on-a-baby-shower-gift/)

## Step 2: Decide how you'll split it

There are two common approaches:

- **Fixed amount:** Everyone pays the same, for example $20 each. It's easy to track and easy to explain.
- **Pay what you can:** People give any amount they choose, and the gift fits whatever comes in. It's kinder to tight budgets, but you won't know your total until the end.

A fixed amount with a quiet "if that's too much, just let me know" is usually the least awkward middle ground. For a deeper look at choosing a rule and sticking with it, see [one simple split rule for group gifts](https://monee-app.com/blog/group-gifts-without-stress-one-simple-split-rule/).

## Step 3: Send one clear message with everything in it

Vague messages get vague replies. Put everything in a single message so nobody has to ask follow-up questions:

> **Hypothetical example:**
> "Hi all! We're chipping in for Sam's farewell gift. It's $20 per person. Please send it by Friday the 6th via [method]. I'll order the gift on Monday with whatever we've collected. Reply 'in' or 'out' so I can plan. No pressure either way!"

That message covers the amount, the deadline, how to pay, when you'll buy, and a polite way to opt out. The opt-out matters more than it seems. People who can say no gracefully are less likely to say yes and then go silent.

## Step 4: Choose a collection method that doesn't cost you money

The best method is the one your group already uses. A few options:

**Peer-to-peer payment apps.** These are built for paying people you know. In the US, Venmo charges [no fee to send money from a Venmo balance, debit card, or bank account, but charges 3% when the sender uses a credit card](https://venmo.com/legal/fees). Since the sender pays that fee, it won't come out of your total. Still, tell people to use a bank account or debit card so nobody gets a surprise charge. Venmo also notes that [card issuers may treat credit card payments as cash advances](https://help.venmo.com/hc/en-us/articles/224361007), which can mean extra fees and a higher interest rate.

**A shared pool.** PayPal relaunched a pools feature in 2024 after [shutting down its original Money Pools in 2021](https://newsroom.uk.paypal-corp.com/evolving-the-ways-you-can-collect-and-raise-money). On PayPal's US help page, [all money in the pool belongs to the pool creator](https://www.paypal.com/us/cshelp/article/what-are-pools-help1197). That means you're responsible for spending it as promised. Which countries have the feature, and how it works, can vary, so check PayPal's page for your market.

**Cash in an envelope.** This works fine for an office or classroom. Write each name on the outside when someone pays so you don't have to rely on memory.

**Everyone buys a piece.** Sometimes nobody has to collect anything. Each person buys one item from a registry or wish list, or the group splits the gift into parts (one person gets the plant, another gets the pot). This takes the most coordination but needs zero money handling.

Whichever method you choose, follow basic payment-app safety. The US National Credit Union Administration advises that these apps are for [people you know and trust, and suggests having the recipient send a payment request first](https://mycredituniongov/about-credit-unions/products-services/mobile-payments-wallets/person-to-person-payment-apps) so money goes to the right account. U.S. PIRG adds that app payments are [usually instant and generally can't be reversed](https://pirg.org/edfund/resources/virtual-wallets-real-complaints/), and that look-alike usernames are a common mix-up. Sending a payment request to each contributor solves both problems, because they just tap "pay."

## Step 5: Set a deadline and buy only with what's in hand

Give people about a week and send one reminder a day or two before the deadline. Then, on buying day:

1. Count what has actually arrived. Pledges don't count.
2. Pick a gift that fits that total, minus a small buffer for tax and shipping.
3. Buy it.

This is the rule that keeps you from fronting the cost. If $160 came in instead of $200, you buy a $150 gift. You don't buy the $200 one and hope the rest shows up.

**A tip for flexible gifts:** a "base gift plus extras" plan makes shortfalls painless. For example, the main gift fits the guaranteed money, and a card, flowers, or a gift card top-up absorbs whatever extra arrives.

## Step 6: Handle the awkward bits

Money between friends and coworkers is about 10% logistics and 90% trying not to sound like a debt collector. Some scripts that help:

- **The late payer:** "Ordering tomorrow! Still want in? Totally fine if not, I just need to know what to buy." This is a question about the gift, not an accusation.
- **The person who pledged and vanished:** After the deadline, quietly drop them from the count and buy within budget. Don't chase them for weeks.
- **The person who wants to pay less:** "Of course, any amount works." Then adjust the gift. It's not worth the friction.
- **The person who wants to swap the gift after you've collected:** "Love that idea for next time!" Then move on.

## Step 7: Keep a simple record

A list with three columns does the job: name, amount, date received. Share the final total and what you bought with the group. Transparency is what makes people happy to chip in next time.

Think of it as a tidy notebook, not a payment tool.

## What to do with leftover money

If more money comes in than you need, you have three good options:

- **Upgrade the gift slightly** and tell everyone.
- **Add it to the card as a gift card** for the recipient.
- **Refund it evenly.** Fair, but often more hassle than the amount is worth.

Tell everyone which one you chose. Unexplained leftover money is how group gifts turn into group gossip.

## Quick checklist

- Agree on a price range before choosing the gift
- Send one message with the amount, method, deadline, buying date, and opt-out
- Use a fee-free payment method, such as bank or debit transfers in the US
- Send payment requests instead of typing in usernames
- Buy only with money already received
- Keep a simple record and share the final total
- Decide what happens to leftovers, and tell everyone
