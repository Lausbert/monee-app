Maybe. It depends on your mortgage servicer. Some will move your due date when you ask. Others won't change the date written in your loan documents. If you can't change the official due date, you can often still pick when your payment goes out, as long as it falls inside your grace period.

The steps below are written for mortgages in the US. Rules and lender practices differ in other countries.

## First, know which "date" you mean

When people ask to change their mortgage payment date, they usually mean one of two things.

**1. The contractual due date.** This is the date written in your promissory note, often the 1st of the month. Moving it means changing your loan terms, and many servicers won't do that. For example, the Massachusetts housing lender MassHousing says in its FAQ that it is ["unable to change the payment due date stated on your Note."](https://www.masshousing.com/maintain-your-home/faq/)

**2. The day you actually pay.** This is the day your autopay draws the money or the day you send the payment yourself. You usually control it, and you can set it anywhere from the due date to the end of your grace period without being charged late.

Most people trying to match their mortgage to payday only need option 2.

## Step 1: Find your due date, grace period, and late fee

Look at your own numbers before you call anyone.

- **Where to find them:** The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) says late fee terms are on [page 4 of your Closing Disclosure](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-are-late-fees-on-a-mortgage-en-1927/) for a loan you're paying now. Your monthly statement and promissory note also show your due date.
- **The grace period:** The CFPB notes that [most mortgage contracts include a grace period](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-are-late-fees-on-a-mortgage-en-1927/). Experian says it's [usually around 15 days](https://www.experian.com/blogs/ask-experian/do-mortgages-have-grace-period/), so a payment due on the 1st can often arrive by the 16th without a fee. Yours may be shorter, so check.
- **The late fee:** Lenders can charge only the amount your mortgage documents allow, and [state law may set a lower limit](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-are-late-fees-on-a-mortgage-en-1927/). Experian puts typical late fees at [3% to 6% of the monthly payment](https://www.experian.com/blogs/ask-experian/do-mortgages-have-grace-period/).
- **Late payments and your credit:** A late fee is not the same as a late mark on your credit report. Experian notes that a missed payment can be [reported as delinquent once it's 30 days past due](https://www.experian.com/blogs/ask-experian/do-mortgages-have-grace-period/).

So your month has three key points: the due date, the end of the grace period, and 30 days past due. Write them down for your own loan.

## Step 2: Decide what problem you're solving

Next, figure out what you actually need. Here's how it breaks down:

| Your situation | Likely fix |
|---|---|
| Payday lands a few days after the due date but before the grace period ends | Move your autopay draft date. No loan change needed. |
| Payday lands after the grace period ends | Ask the servicer to change the contractual due date. |
| Money is tight every month, whatever the date | Ask the servicer about hardship options, not just a new date. |

**A hypothetical example:** Say you're paid on the 15th and the last day of each month. Your mortgage is due on the 1st, with a grace period through the 16th. You could leave the due date alone and set autopay for the 2nd. That way the money from your end-of-month paycheck has a day or two to clear before the payment goes out.

If several bills clash with your pay schedule, this guide on [how to move bill due dates to match your paydays](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-move-bill-due-dates-to-match-your-paydays/) covers the whole picture.

## Step 3: Call your servicer with specific questions

Your servicer is the company that sends your statement. It may not be the lender you originally borrowed from. When you call, go through these questions:

1. Do you change contractual due dates? If so, which dates can I choose?
2. Is there a fee, or will my interest change for the month I switch?
3. When does the new date start, and what do I owe before then?
4. If you won't change the due date, can I set my autopay for any day in the grace period?
5. Can you confirm the change in writing?

Question 3 matters because mortgages are paid in arrears, which means each payment covers interest for the month before. Moving the date can mean a partial-month interest adjustment. MCAP, a Canadian lender, says borrowers who change their payment date should review [the effects on their maturity date, plus any fees or interest adjustment](https://www.mcap.com/mymcap/help/mortgage-changes/mortgage-changes-info/change-due-date-of-mortgage-payments). That's a Canadian example, but the same questions apply in the US.

**If someone offers you a biweekly payment program,** ask about fees before signing up. In the US, the CFPB sued one biweekly payment company, saying it charged consumers [setup fees of up to $995 plus yearly processing fees](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-files-suit-against-nationwide-biweekly-for-luring-consumers-with-false-promises-of-mortgage-savings/). Ask your servicer what free options it offers.

**If your loan was recently transferred,** you have some short-term protection. Under US federal rules, for 60 days after a servicing transfer, the new servicer can't charge a late fee or treat a payment as late if you sent it on time to the old servicer. The FTC explains this in its guide to [your rights when paying your mortgage](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/your-rights-when-paying-your-mortgage). Check your due date with the new servicer anyway.

## Step 4: Plan the switch month and add a buffer

Whether you move the due date or just the autopay date, plan the first month carefully.

- **Watch for overlap.** The switch month may bring a payment due soon after your last one, or an interest adjustment. Find out the exact amount and date ahead of time.
- **Keep paying until it's confirmed.** Keep paying on the old schedule until you have the change in writing.
- **Update your autopay.** If you pay through your bank's bill pay instead of the servicer's autopay, change the date there too. Leave enough time for the payment to arrive.
- **Plan around holidays.** If your payment date lands on a weekend or bank holiday, the payment can be delayed. This guide shows [how to budget for bank holidays and payment delays](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-budget-for-bank-holidays-and-payment-delays/).
- **Keep a small cushion.** Leave some extra money in the account your payment comes from, so a late paycheck doesn't turn into a late payment. The same approach is explained in this [due-date, autopay, and buffer plan](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-avoid-credit-card-late-fees-with-a-duedate-autopay-buffer-plan/).

You can also turn this around. Instead of moving the bill, start your budget month on payday. This guide explains [how to pick a budget month start date that saves fees](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-pick-a-budget-month-start-date-that-saves-fees/).

## The short version

- Look up your due date, grace period, and late fee in your Closing Disclosure and monthly statement.
- Decide whether you need a new contractual due date or just a different autopay date.
- Ask your servicer about fees, interest adjustments, and start dates, and get the answers in writing.
- Plan the switch month, keep paying on the old schedule until the change is confirmed, and keep a small cushion in your account.

Even if your servicer won't change the due date, your grace period usually gives you room to pick a payment date that works for you.
