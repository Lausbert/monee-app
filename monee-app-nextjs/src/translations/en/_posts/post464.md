You can stop boredom spending by deciding what to do with bored time before it arrives. A free-fun list is a short set of no-cost activities written down ahead of time. When the urge to browse and buy shows up, you don't have to rely on willpower. You just pick something from the list. It works because boredom usually isn't a need for a new *thing*. It's a need for something to do, and you can meet that need without paying.

This guide explains why boredom leads to spending, how to build a list you'll actually use, and how to set up your phone and accounts so the list becomes the easier choice.

## Why boredom turns into spending

If you shop when you're bored, plenty of other people do too. In a [Motley Fool survey of 2,000 US adults (May 2025)](https://chainstoreage.com/survey-top-reasons-consumers-overspend-include), 45% of Gen Z respondents said boredom is when they most often spend money wastefully, compared with 30% of baby boomers. The same survey found that about half of millennials and Gen Z regret most of their impulse purchases.

Academic research points the same way. A [2022 study in *Frontiers in Psychology*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.918306/full) using German and Turkish samples found that boredom proneness strongly predicted online procrastination, which included online shopping. The authors also noted that boredom is a strong motivation for visiting online stores. A [2020 field study of 313 young consumers in Turkey](https://acikerisim.kku.edu.tr/items/92174678-2446-4783-ad13-0f833293ae2f) found that boredom proneness affected impulse purchasing, partly through smartphone use.

These are small or survey-based studies, so read them as patterns rather than hard rules. Still, the pattern is clear: boredom makes you look for stimulation, your phone is always nearby, and shopping apps are built to give you that quick hit. A free-fun list puts a different, free source of stimulation in the same spot.

## How to build your free-fun list

### 1. Notice your boredom windows

Over a week or two, write down when you tend to browse or buy without a plan. Common times include:

- Late evenings on the couch
- Sunday afternoons with nothing scheduled
- Lunch breaks
- Waiting rooms, commutes or queues

Your list should cover the moments you actually face, not an imaginary free Saturday.

### 2. Sort activities by time and energy

Bored-and-tired calls for different activities than bored-and-restless. Make short sections so you can choose fast:

- **5 minutes or less:** for queues and quick breaks
- **15–30 minutes:** for lunch breaks or evenings
- **An hour or more:** for weekends
- **Low energy:** things you can do on the couch
- **High energy:** things that get you moving

### 3. Choose activities that give you what shopping gives you

Think about what browsing does for you. Maybe it's novelty, a sense of anticipation, a small reward or the feeling of making something better. Then pick free activities that give you the same feeling:

| If shopping gives you… | Try a free swap like… |
|---|---|
| Novelty | Walk a street or trail you've never been on |
| Anticipation | Place a hold on a library book and wait for it |
| A "treat" feeling | Make a favorite drink at home and sit outside |
| Self-improvement | Follow a free online workout or language lesson |
| Social connection | Call or message a friend you've lost touch with |
| Creativity | Sketch, write, cook from what's in the pantry |

### 4. Write it down where the urge happens

Keep the list where you'll see it when you're bored. That might be a note pinned on your phone's home screen, a card on the fridge, or a widget next to where your shopping apps used to be. A list you have to go looking for won't help in the moment.

### 5. Keep it short and fresh

About 10 to 20 items is plenty. Too many options can feel like one more chore. Every month or so, cross off anything you keep skipping and add something new.

## Free-fun ideas to start your list

Use these as a starting point and keep the ones that fit your life.

**Quick (under 15 minutes)**
- Stretch or do a short bodyweight routine
- Tidy one drawer or shelf
- Text someone a genuine compliment
- Step outside and walk around the block
- Play a free puzzle or word game that has no in-app purchases

**Medium (15–60 minutes)**
- Borrow an ebook or audiobook through your public library. With a library card, the [Libby app by OverDrive](https://www.overdrive.com/apps/libby) lets you borrow from participating libraries for free, and titles return automatically.
- Cook something new using only ingredients you already have
- Rearrange a room or redo a corner of your home
- Start a journal, a sketchbook or a short-story draft
- Watch a free lecture or tutorial on a skill you want to learn

**Longer (an hour or more)**
- Visit a park, beach, trail or public garden near you
- Check your local library or community center for free events
- Host a potluck, game night or movie night at home
- Volunteer locally
- Plan a "no-spend day" with a friend and explore your own town

Local offers vary a lot by country and city, so look at what your library, council or community groups actually run near you.

## Make the list easier to pick than the cart

A list works best when spending takes a bit more effort. These changes add a pause without banning anything:

- **Remove saved cards** from shopping sites and browsers, so you have to type your card number each time.
- **Log out of or delete shopping apps** you open out of habit. You can still shop on a browser when you really need something.
- **Unsubscribe from promotional emails and turn off store notifications.** Each one is a nudge to start browsing.
- **Use a wait-list.** Put anything you want into a note or wishlist and come back after 24 to 72 hours. If you still want it and it fits your budget, buy it on purpose.
- **Ask yourself one question:** "Am I bored, or do I need this?" If the honest answer is "bored," open your list.

## Track what changes

You don't need a complicated system. Once a week, look at your spending and note which purchases happened in your boredom windows. If you use a budgeting app or a spreadsheet, give unplanned purchases their own category so you can see whether the amount drops over time. Small wins count. Skipping a few unplanned orders in a month shows the habit is shifting.

It also helps to give the money you didn't spend a job. Moving it into savings or toward a goal makes the progress feel real, not like something you're just missing out on.

## When it's more than boredom

Sometimes the urge to buy is about stress, loneliness or low mood rather than boredom. If shopping feels hard to control, causes debt or conflict, or leaves you feeling distressed, think about talking to a doctor, mental health professional or nonprofit credit counselor in your country. Reaching out is a practical step, and nothing to be ashamed of.

## The bottom line

Boredom spending is a habit, and you can change habits once you see the pattern. Notice when the urge shows up and decide ahead of time what you'll do instead. Then make that choice easier than tapping "buy." A short, honest free-fun list in the right spot takes the decision off your plate when you're bored and gives you something better to do.
