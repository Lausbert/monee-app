Your new job has started, but your first paycheck can be weeks away. To budget for that gap, find the exact date your first pay will land. Then add up every cost you have to pay before that date, compare the total with the cash you have now, and close any shortfall in this order: cut costs, delay bills, then borrow cheaply. Whatever happens, steer clear of high-cost loans.

The rest of this guide goes through each step. It's meant for the week when you're also buying work shoes, sorting out new commutes, and wondering where the grocery money went.

## Step 1: Find your real first payday

Don't guess this date. Ask HR or your manager three questions:

1. **How often does the company pay?** Weekly, every two weeks, twice a month, or monthly. In the US, every two weeks (biweekly) is the most common schedule: [43.0% of private establishments used it in February 2023, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/ces/publications/length-pay-period.htm).
2. **When does the current pay period end, and when is that period paid?** Many employers pay "in arrears," which means you're paid some days after a pay period ends. If you start partway through a period, your first payday may come later than you expect.
3. **Will the first check be a full one?** A partial first pay period, tax withholding, and new benefit deductions can all make your first check smaller than your normal take-home pay.

In the US, pay-frequency rules are set mostly by each state, not by federal law. The [US Department of Labor's state payday table](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/state/payday) shows the minimum pay frequency for each state, and the Labor Department itself says to check with your state labor office for official details. If you're outside the US, check your own country's employment rules or your employment contract.

**Hypothetical example:** You start on a Wednesday. Your employer pays every two weeks, a week after each period closes. The period you join ends 10 days after your start date, so your first payday is about 17 days away. That first check covers only 8 working days, not a full two weeks. So you have to cover more than two weeks of living costs, and that check alone won't cover your next stretch of bills either.

## Step 2: Count the days and list every cost in them

Write down how many days are left until payday. Then list everything you'll pay before that date, sorted into three groups.

**Fixed bills with due dates**
- Rent or mortgage
- Utilities, phone, internet
- Insurance premiums
- Minimum debt payments
- Childcare or school fees

**Daily living costs**
- Groceries
- Gas, transit passes, parking
- Household basics and kids' needs

**New-job costs** (these are easy to forget)
- Work clothes or required gear
- A new commute route or parking permit
- Lunches during the first week while you learn the area
- A deposit or extra care hours if your schedule changed

For daily costs, use a per-day figure. Take what you usually spend on groceries and transport in a normal week, divide by 7, and multiply by the number of days until payday. That's usually more accurate than guessing one big number.

If you've also just moved, this [7-category first apartment budget template](https://monee-app.com/blog/first-apartment-budget-a-7category-starter-template/) can help you catch costs you'd otherwise miss.

## Step 3: Compare the gap total with the money you actually have

Add up the cash you can actually reach between now and payday:

- Checking account balance
- Savings you're willing to use
- A final paycheck or unused leave payout from your old job, but only if you know the date and amount
- Any refunds or reimbursements you're owed

Then use this calculation:

**Available cash − costs until payday = your gap**

If the number is positive, you're covered. Keep a small cushion and don't spend the extra. If it's negative, that's your shortfall, and Step 4 is about closing it.

## Step 4: Close the shortfall in the right order

Start at the top. Move down the list only if the shortfall is still there.

### 1. Cut costs that are flexible for a few weeks

- **Use up the food you already have.** Plan meals around your pantry and freezer before buying anything new. A [use-first shopping list](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-budget-for-food-waste-with-a-usefirst-list/) makes this easier.
- **Pause subscriptions** and memberships you can restart later.
- **Delay purchases that can wait.** The new desk chair or a sofa upgrade can wait until the second paycheck. When you do buy, a [used-first approach to furnishing](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-furnish-a-home-on-a-budget-with-a-usedfirst-rule/) keeps costs down.
- **Buy only what the first week needs.** You probably don't need a whole new wardrobe, just enough to get through until payday.

### 2. Move due dates instead of borrowing

Call before a bill is due, not after. Many landlords, utilities, and lenders will agree to a new due date or a short payment arrangement, especially if you can show a job offer letter with a pay date. Get any agreement in writing.

### 3. Ask your employer

Some employers offer a pay advance for new hires or can process your first check early. It doesn't hurt to ask HR politely. Some employers also offer earned wage access, which lets you draw pay you've already earned before payday. Fees and terms vary a lot, so compare them with this [cost-speed matrix for early paycheck apps](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-decide-if-earned-wage-access-early-paycheck-apps-are-worth-it-with-a-costspeed-matrix/) before you sign up.

### 4. Borrow small and cheap, if you have to

If you still have a shortfall, low-cost options come first. That might be a small loan from family with a clear repayment date, or a credit card you can pay off in full when your check arrives. Avoid payday loans if you can. In the US, the [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-are-the-costs-and-fees-for-a-payday-loan-en-1589/) says a common payday loan fee of $15 per $100 borrowed works out to an annual percentage rate of almost 400 percent for a two-week loan.

## Step 5: Plan the first paycheck before it arrives

The gap doesn't fully close on your first payday, especially if that first check is a partial one. Decide in advance where the money goes:

1. **Pay back anything you borrowed** or deferred during the gap.
2. **Cover the bills due before your second paycheck.**
3. **Restock your buffer**, even by a small amount, so the next new job or surprise bill hurts less.
4. **Then** catch up on the purchases you put off.

Some budgeting apps let you set your budget month to start on payday instead of the 1st, which can make this easier to see. If your hours or pay change from check to check while you settle in, this [paycheck budgeting playbook for irregular income](https://monee-app.com/blog/paycheck-budgeting-for-irregular-income-a-practical-playbook/) can help you plan for the next few months.

## Quick gap-budget checklist

- [ ] Confirm your exact first payday and whether the check covers a partial period
- [ ] Count the days until payday
- [ ] List fixed bills due before payday
- [ ] Work out daily costs (weekly spending ÷ 7 × days)
- [ ] Add new-job costs
- [ ] Add up the cash you can actually reach
- [ ] Close any shortfall: cut, defer, ask your employer, then borrow cheaply
- [ ] Plan your first paycheck before it lands

The gap before your first paycheck only lasts a few weeks. A dated plan covers it and keeps you out of expensive debt.
