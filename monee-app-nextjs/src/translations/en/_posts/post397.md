My “cheap” commute starts costing me money before I even reach the first traffic light. The surprise is not that cycling has expenses—it is how easily I ignore them while congratulating myself for being financially sensible.

I start cycling to work regularly during a stretch of suspiciously cooperative Cologne weather. The route is manageable, the morning air wakes me up, and I arrive feeling as if I have already completed one responsible adult task.

Best of all, I stop paying for daily public transport.

At least, that is the story I tell myself.

Then my rear tyre goes flat on a wet Tuesday. A few days later, the brakes begin making a noise that sounds less like a safety feature and more like an experimental jazz performance. At the repair shop, I agree to several sensible replacements while mentally comparing the bill with all the fares I have supposedly avoided.

For the first time, I wonder: Is cycling actually cheaper, or does it simply spread the costs out more creatively?

## I Start Tracking the Whole Commute

My first instinct is to compare transport tickets with bicycle repairs. That makes cycling look good, but it is not a fair test.

A bike commute needs more than a functioning bike. I have lights, a lock, weatherproof trousers, gloves, a helmet and a bag that does not surrender immediately in the rain. None of these purchases feels dramatic on its own. Together, they represent more than I expected.

So I create a “cycling to work” category in Monee and begin recording anything connected to the commute. I am not trying to punish myself for buying chain oil. I am getting curious about my own patterns.

The category includes:

- Repairs and replacement parts
- Basic maintenance supplies
- Rain and safety gear
- Occasional public transport when the weather becomes unreasonable
- Extra laundry caused by arriving damp or muddy
- The small convenience purchases I make because cycling changes my routine

That last category is revealing. On cycling mornings, I sometimes leave home earlier and buy breakfast near the studio. The bike does not force me to buy a pastry, obviously. It is not steering me into the bakery. But the routine creates the opportunity, and apparently I am very vulnerable to opportunities containing butter.

## The Result Is Less Obvious Than I Expect

After several months, cycling is still cheaper than relying on public transport for every journey—but not by the huge margin I had imagined.

The biggest difference depends on the bike itself. Mine is already paid for, practical and not particularly attractive to thieves. If I had bought a new commuter bike, expensive accessories and specialist clothing all at once, the first year would look very different.

Repairs also arrive unevenly. I can go weeks spending nothing, then replace several worn parts together. That makes cycling feel free until it suddenly feels expensive.

Public transport is the opposite. The cost is predictable and visible. Cycling hides its bill in small shops, online orders and one memorable conversation with a mechanic.

Still, the total-cost test reveals something important: the savings grow over time. Most gear lasts beyond one season, and regular maintenance is far cheaper than repeatedly fixing neglected problems. Once I stop treating the bicycle as a magical zero-cost machine, I make better decisions about it.

## What I Would Do Differently

I would not buy all the “essential” cycling gear immediately. Some items genuinely improve safety and comfort. Others solve problems I do not actually have.

I would also create a small maintenance buffer from the beginning. Repairs feel less annoying when I expect them, and I am less tempted to delay a worn brake pad until it becomes an exciting personality test.

Most importantly, I would compare realistic alternatives. If I already pay for a monthly transport pass, cycling may not reduce that expense. If biking lets me avoid the pass entirely, the calculation changes. Distance, storage, weather and shower access matter just as much as enthusiasm.

## What the Test Taught Me

My practical takeaways are simple:

1. Count setup costs, repairs and backup transport—not just fuel or fares.
2. Spread long-lasting gear costs across the time you expect to use them.
3. Track convenience spending created by the new routine.
4. Budget for maintenance before something breaks.
5. Compare cycling with the transport option you would genuinely use instead.

If you are in this situation, there are three honest outcomes: cycling saves money immediately, it becomes cheaper over time, or it costs roughly the same but gives you benefits you value more.

For me, cycling wins—but by a smaller, muddier and much more believable margin than I first claimed.
