For most people, last year's model is worth buying. There are two conditions. The features it lacks can't be ones you'd use every week, and it still needs plenty of years of software support left. If both are true, the discount is basically free money. If either one fails, the "deal" turns into a slow tax you pay later.

This guide walks you through a quick feature-gap test so you can decide on the facts. That matters most when the money comes from a shared account and your partner asks, very calmly, why a phone costs as much as a weekend away.

## Why last year's model is often the smart buy

New flagship models usually bring small changes: a slightly better camera, a faster chip, maybe a new button nobody asked for. The older model often drops in price or gets cheaper through refurbished and open-box channels, and it does most of the same things.

The big change in recent years is how long phones now get updates:

- **Google Pixel:** Pixel 8 and later phones "will get updates for 7 years starting from when the device first became available on the Google Store in the US," covering OS and security updates, [according to Google's support page](https://support.google.com/pixelphone/answer/4457705?hl=en).
- **Samsung Galaxy:** Samsung promised the Galaxy S24 series seven generations of Android upgrades and seven years of security updates, [as Engadget reported at launch](https://www.engadget.com/samsung-pledges-seven-years-of-updates-for-s24-series-180844109.html). Samsung's fine print says timing and availability can vary by model and market.

So a flagship that's one year old may still have around six years of support left. That's longer than many people keep a phone anyway.

The catch is that support windows differ by generation. Google gives the Pixel 6 and 7 series five years of updates, not seven, [per the same Google page](https://support.google.com/pixelphone/answer/4457705?hl=en). "Old" can mean "still great" or "near the end," and the price tag won't tell you which.

## The feature-gap test (5 questions)

Answer these before you buy. It takes about ten minutes and saves a lot of "but the new one has…" talk later.

### 1. What exactly is different?

Open the maker's comparison page and list every real difference between the two models. Skip the marketing words. You want plain facts: chip, camera sensors, battery size, display, charging speed, storage options, and software features.

Most years, that list is shorter than you'd expect.

### 2. Would you use each difference weekly?

Go through the list and mark each item:

- **Weekly:** you'd notice it all the time (battery life, camera in low light, screen brightness outdoors)
- **Sometimes:** nice to have (faster charging, a new zoom level)
- **Never:** you'll forget it exists within a month

If nothing lands in "weekly," last year's model is almost certainly the better buy.

### 3. How many years of support are left?

Find the official update policy and work out the end date from the original launch date, not from the day you buy. Then subtract the time that has already passed.

A simple rule of thumb: if the remaining support is shorter than the time you plan to keep the device, the discount has to be big enough to cover replacing it sooner.

### 4. What's the real price gap?

Compare the total cost of each option, not just the sticker price:

- Purchase price
- Case, charger, or accessories you'd need anyway
- Warranty or protection plan
- Trade-in value of your current device (sometimes better on the new model due to promos)

A hypothetical example: the new model costs $200 more, and the only "weekly" difference is a slightly better night camera. Ask yourself whether you'd pay $200 for a camera upgrade on its own. If not, you have your answer.

### 5. Is the seller's warranty solid?

This matters most if you're buying refurbished or open-box. Warranty terms differ a lot. [Consumer Reports found](https://www.consumerreports.org/electronics-computers/should-you-buy-refurbished-electronics-a9384325045/) that Apple, Bose, Microsoft, and Samsung covered their own refurbished items for a full year, while some big retailers offered only 90 days. Return windows varied too. Policies change, so check the current terms before you pay.

The [FTC also notes](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/warranties) that you have a right to read the warranty before you buy, whether you shop in person or online. If specific parts or repairs aren't listed, assume they aren't covered.

## When the new model is actually worth it

Last year's model isn't always the winner. Paying more for the new one can make sense if:

- **A "weekly" feature is on the list.** If you take lots of photos in dim light, or your battery dies by 4 p.m., a real upgrade there may be worth the price.
- **Support is running short.** An older-generation device with only a year or two of updates left is a poor long-term buy, however cheap it looks.
- **You keep devices for a very long time.** If you plan to use it until it physically falls apart, the extra year of support on the newer model adds real value.
- **The price gap is tiny.** Launch promos and trade-in deals sometimes shrink the difference to almost nothing. Then the newer model wins by default.

## How to talk about it without a fight

Tech purchases from shared money come with their own awkward moments. One person says "investment." The other says "you literally have a phone." Both have a point.

A few things that keep the conversation calm:

- **Agree on a "no-questions" limit.** Many couples set an amount each person can spend without a group discussion. Anything above it gets a quick chat. A new flagship phone is usually above it.
- **Bring the test, not the vibes.** "It's newer" won't convince anyone. "The battery lasts longer and I'm charging twice a day" might.
- **Decide whose money it is.** If you use a "yours, mine, and ours" setup, a personal gadget may come from personal money. Monee's guide to [budgeting when you move in together](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-budget-for-moving-in-together-without-fights/) explains this setup alongside equal and income-based splits.
- **Count the savings too.** If you pick last year's model and save $200, decide together where that money goes. It could go to the vacation fund, the emergency fund, or just toward being right.

If you track shared spending, logging a big purchase like this keeps it from turning into a mystery later. [Monee](https://monee-app.com/) lets partners or roommates log expenses into a shared household and see the same numbers, without linking a bank account. You enter each expense by hand, which takes a few seconds per entry and also makes you notice what you're spending.

## Quick checklist

Before you buy, you should be able to answer yes to these:

- [ ] I listed the real differences between the two models.
- [ ] None of the missing features is something I'd use every week, or I've decided it's worth paying for.
- [ ] The older model has more years of support left than I plan to keep it.
- [ ] I compared the total cost, including trade-ins and accessories.
- [ ] I read the warranty and return policy, especially for refurbished or open-box devices.
- [ ] If it's shared money, we both agreed on it (ideally before it arrived at the door).

If most of these are yes, last year's model is probably the smart buy. You get nearly the same device, years of updates, and money left for something else.
