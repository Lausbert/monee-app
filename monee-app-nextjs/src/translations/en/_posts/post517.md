Yes, usually. In most homes an electric blanket costs far less to run than heating the whole house. There's one condition: **you only save money if you also turn the thermostat down.** A blanket warms you. Your furnace or boiler warms every room, every wall and the air in between. If the heat stays at the same setting and you add a blanket, your bill goes up a little.

The rest of this guide shows how to run the numbers for your own home, what the savings might look like, and how to use a heated blanket safely.

## What an electric blanket costs to run

Electric blankets use very little power. Depending on the model, most draw somewhere between about 40 and 150 watts, according to [UK supplier EDF](https://www.edfenergy.com/node/120804). Some sources give higher figures for heated throws. The wattage on the label is the maximum. On a lower setting, the blanket uses less.

Here's the formula:

**Watts × hours used ÷ 1,000 × your price per kWh = cost**

**Hypothetical example (US):** A 100-watt blanket runs for 8 hours a night. That's 0.8 kWh. The US Energy Information Administration expects the average US home electricity price to be [about 18 cents per kWh in 2026](https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/tables/pdf/SF02.pdf). So:

- 0.8 kWh × $0.18 = about **14 cents a night**
- About **$4.30 for 30 nights**
- About **$22 for a 150-night heating season**

Your rate may be very different. US state averages range from about 11 cents to over 40 cents per kWh, so check the price per kWh on your own bill.

In the UK, EDF works out that a 70-watt blanket costs about 1.84p an hour at a rate of 26.32p per kWh. That's roughly 15p for eight hours.

## What turning down the heat can save

Heating is the biggest energy cost in most homes. In the US, space heating made up [42% of household energy use in 2020](https://www.eia.gov/pressroom/releases/press535.php), and the average household spent $519 a year on it. The cost depended heavily on the fuel: about $396 for electric heat, $519 for natural gas, $1,010 for propane and $1,164 for fuel oil.

How much can a lower setting save? The US Department of Energy says you can save [as much as 10% a year on heating and cooling](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/thermostats) by turning your thermostat back 7–10°F for 8 hours a day. Doing it overnight is the easy option, since you're already in bed.

In the UK, the Energy Saving Trust estimated that turning a room thermostat down from 22°C to 21°C [could save about £105 a year](https://energysavingtrust.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/240122-Home-energy-saving-behaviors.pdf), based on February 2024 prices.

## Putting the two together

**Hypothetical example (US):** Your home spends $519 a year on heating, the 2020 national average. Energy prices have gone up since then, so your real figure is probably higher. You use an electric blanket and turn the thermostat down 8°F overnight.

| | Yearly estimate |
|---|---|
| Heating savings (up to 10%) | up to about $52 |
| Blanket running cost (150 nights) | about $22 |
| **Net savings** | **up to about $30** |

That's a modest saving. It grows if:

- Your heating fuel is expensive, like propane or fuel oil
- Your electricity is cheap
- You live somewhere with long, cold winters
- You also lower the heat during the day while you're out or bundled up on the sofa

It shrinks, or disappears, if:

- You don't actually lower the thermostat
- Several people each need their own blanket
- Your heating is already set low

Don't forget the price of the blanket itself. To see how long it takes to pay for itself, divide the price by your yearly net savings. The same approach works for many home purchases. This [simple break-even test](https://monee-app.com/blog/are-refill-products-cheaper-a-simple-break-even-test/) walks through it step by step.

## How to check this for your own home

1. **Find your electricity rate.** Look for the price per kWh on your latest bill.
2. **Check the blanket's wattage.** It's on the label or in the product listing.
3. **Estimate your winter heating cost.** Add up your gas, oil, propane or electric bills for the cold months, then subtract a typical summer month to remove other uses.
4. **Choose a realistic overnight setting.** A 7–10°F drop (roughly 4–5°C) is the range the DOE uses.
5. **Compare for one month.** Try the new routine for a full billing cycle. Then compare your usage (kWh or therms, not just dollars) with the same month last year, keeping in mind that the weather may have been different.

If you're home during the day, it's worth running the same check on daytime heating too. This guide on whether [working from home is actually cheaper](https://monee-app.com/blog/is-working-from-home-actually-cheaper-a-simple-test/) helps you see what those extra heating hours cost.

## When an electric blanket won't save much

- **You have a heat pump.** Big overnight drops can make some heat pumps switch on backup resistance heat to warm the house back up, and that can cancel out the savings. Ask your installer or check the manual about what setback works best for your system.
- **Your home is hard to heat back up.** A drafty or poorly insulated home may stay cold for a while in the morning. Smaller setbacks may suit you better.
- **Other people in the home need it warmer.** Babies, older adults and anyone with health conditions affected by cold may need warmer rooms. A blanket only helps the person under it.
- **Very cold weather.** Don't lower the heat so far that pipes are at risk of freezing.

## Use it safely

An electric blanket is only a good deal if it's safe. Fire safety groups and insurers point to a few key habits:

- **Replace old blankets.** [State Farm](https://www.statefarm.com/simple-insights/safety/the-dos-and-donts-of-electric-blanket-safety) suggests throwing out blankets that are ten years old or more, because the wiring inside can wear out even when it looks fine.
- **Plug it straight into the wall.** The [Electrical Safety Foundation International](https://www.esfi.org/electric-blanket-fire-prevention/) advises against using extension cords or power strips with electric blankets.
- **Don't fold it or tuck it in while it's on.** Bunched-up blankets can overheat.
- **Look for a timer or auto shut-off.** Many modern blankets have one. Follow the maker's instructions on whether it's safe to leave on overnight. If you're unsure, warm the bed first and switch the blanket off before you go to sleep.
- **Don't buy used.** [Cheshire Fire and Rescue Service](https://www.cheshirefire.gov.uk/your-safety/home-safety/electrical-fire-safety/electric-blankets) in the UK warns against secondhand blankets and says to stop using any blanket that shows damage or scorch marks.

## The bottom line

An electric blanket is one of the cheapest ways to stay warm at night. It costs a few cents a night, while heating a whole house costs far more. But the blanket is only half of it. The savings come from turning the heat down. Make that one change, check your next bill, and you'll find out what it's worth in your home.
