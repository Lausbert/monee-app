**Short answer:** In the US, **$15 to $30** is a common amount for a classmate's birthday gift, and **about $25** sits in the middle. If your child barely knows the birthday kid, $10–$20 is fine. For a close friend you can spend more if you want to. Whatever the amount, choose one you can repeat for every invitation that comes in this year.

## What experts and surveys say

There is very little formal research on gifts for classmates in particular. Most of the guidance comes from etiquette experts and small polls:

- **Etiquette experts (US):** Reviewed calls **$25** the "sweet spot" for a classmate or friend. Etiquette coach Jennifer Porter says ["you can't go wrong if you spend around $20 to $30"](https://reviewed.com/parenting/features/how-much-money-to-spend-on-a-kids-birthday-gift). This is expert advice, not survey data.
- **A small parent poll (US):** What to Expect ran a small poll on no-gift parties. According to the [Deseret News](https://www.deseret.com/family/2024/08/06/no-gift-kids-birthday-party-etiquette/), the average gift cost **about $28**. The article doesn't say how many parents took part, so treat the figure as a rough signal.
- **A national survey of "going rates" (US):** Empower surveyed 2,202 US adults in August 2025. Respondents named [$83.03 as the going rate for a child's birthday gift and $55.65 for an adult's](https://www.empower.com/node/14261). That question asked about any child, including your own kids or close family. It wasn't about classmates, so the number is much higher than typical party-gift advice. The same survey found that **86%** agree a gift can be meaningful without being expensive.

These sources point to roughly $20–$30 as a normal amount for a classmate. Spending less is also fine. Nobody at the party checks prices.

## A simple guide by situation

These rough ranges reflect US norms. Adjust them for your own currency and local prices.

| Situation | Typical range (US) |
|---|---|
| Whole-class party, child barely knows the birthday kid | $10–$20 |
| Regular classmate or casual friend | $15–$25 |
| Good friend from school | $20–$35 |
| Very close friend or a close friend's or relative's child | More, if you choose (up to about $100) |

The "up to $100" figure comes from Helen Holden, founder of the party-planning site Counting Candles. She told Reviewed you [can spend up to $100 on the child of a close friend or relative](https://reviewed.com/parenting/features/how-much-money-to-spend-on-a-kids-birthday-gift). That's an option, not an expectation.

**Is a $30 gift too much for a classmate?** No. At around $30, a toy like a doll, a building set or a board game is at the top of the usual range. It suits a good friend or a family that can comfortably spend that much. For a classmate your child hardly knows, a cheaper gift is just as appropriate.

## Start with a few questions

Before you settle on an amount, think about these:

- **How many parties does your child go to in a year?** One or two invitations feel very different from one almost every weekend.
- **How close is the friendship?** A child your kid talks about every day is different from someone they've barely met.
- **What do families in your community usually do?** Expectations vary between schools, neighborhoods and countries, so local norms are worth noticing. You don't have to follow them.
- **What matters most to you?** Maybe it's being generous, keeping things simple, teaching your child about thoughtful giving, or protecting a tight budget. Any of these is a good reason for your choice.

## Gift or cash?

A physical gift is the norm at most kids' parties. Cash or a gift card in a card is also acceptable, and the same ranges apply. Some families ask for this directly with a "fiver" party. Reviewed describes these parties as ones where [guests bring $5 in a card](https://reviewed.com/parenting/features/how-much-money-to-spend-on-a-kids-birthday-gift), and the birthday child puts the total toward one bigger gift or saves it. If an invitation asks for something specific, such as no gifts, a book swap or a set cash amount, follow it.

## Why a consistent amount helps

If you pick a standard amount and stick to it, you make one decision instead of a new one before every party. It also feels fair across all your child's friends, and it keeps gift spending predictable.

To see your yearly total, multiply your usual gift amount by the number of parties you expect. Here's a hypothetical example: 12 parties at $20 each comes to $240 a year, before cards or wrapping. If that number surprises you, you can [set a yearly birthday gift fund](https://monee-app.com/blog/birthday-budget-set-a-yearly-gift-fund-without-guessing/) so the money is already set aside when the invitations arrive. The same approach works for other school-year costs, such as [end-of-year teacher gifts](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-much-should-you-spend-on-a-teacher-gift/).

## Ways to give well for less

A lower budget doesn't have to mean a worse gift. Some options:

- **Keep a gift stash.** Buying a few good gifts on sale ahead of time spares you last-minute store runs, which usually cost more.
- **Choose something to use up.** Art supplies, a book, a craft kit or a card game are usually welcome and don't add clutter. Porter suggests [active gifts that will get used](https://reviewed.com/parenting/features/how-much-money-to-spend-on-a-kids-birthday-gift), such as a STEM kit, a board game or a sports game.
- **Use gift cards you already have.** Store credit you haven't spent can cover part of a present. It helps to [keep track of your gift cards and store credits](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-track-gift-cards-and-store-credits-without-losing-money/) so they don't expire unused.
- **Go in on a group gift.** Some classes or friend groups pool money for one bigger present. If you're organizing one, a [simple split rule for group gifts](https://monee-app.com/blog/group-gifts-without-stress-one-simple-split-rule/) keeps it fair and low-stress.
- **Add a personal touch.** A handmade card from your child often means more to the birthday kid than a few extra dollars.

## What if other parents spend more?

Some gifts at the party may cost much more than yours. That's fine. Children rarely compare prices, and most parents are simply glad their child's friend came. A thoughtful gift within your budget is a perfectly good gift.

If you're hosting, the same thinking applies the other way. Guests' budgets aren't your concern. More hosts now write "no gifts" on the invitation: the Deseret News reports that [as many as 1 in 8 birthday invitations now say so](https://www.deseret.com/family/2024/08/06/no-gift-kids-birthday-party-etiquette/), and about half of parents in What to Expect's poll either didn't want gifts or didn't mind whether guests brought them. If you're planning a party yourself, see [what a kids' birthday party typically costs](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-much-to-spend-on-a-kids-birthday-party/).

## The bottom line

In the US, $15 to $30 is a common and reasonable amount for a classmate's birthday gift, with about $25 as a typical middle. Spending less is still fine. Pick an amount you can keep up with all year, use it for every party, and save bigger gifts for close friends and family.
