**The short answer:** to ask for a discount, say what you want plainly and politely, give one short reason, ask an open question, and then stop talking. For example:

> "Hi, I'm reviewing my expenses and this cost is higher than I'd like. Is there any discount, lower rate or better plan available?"

That sentence works because it's calm and direct. It doesn't over-explain or apologize, and it gives the other person something clear to answer. The rest of this guide gives you scripts for phone, chat, email and in-person requests, plus replies for the usual objections.

## Why asking feels awkward (and why it's worth it anyway)

Most people don't skip asking because they're careless with money. They skip it because the moment feels uncomfortable. What if they say no? What if I sound cheap? Research backs this up. A 2025 study by researchers at Indiana University, Cornell and Leuphana University, published in *Negotiation and Conflict Management Research*, found that 95% of participants chose not to negotiate up to 51% of the time. Over half of buyers would pay 5% to 11% more rather than bargain ([Newswise / Indiana University, 2025](https://www.newswise.com/articles/why-many-americans-avoid-negotiating-even-when-it-costs-them/?sc=c6262)).

People who do ask often get something. These surveys are all from the US and some are a few years old, so treat them as signals, not guarantees:

- In a 2018 Consumer Reports survey of TV and internet bundle customers, 70% had tried to negotiate. Of those, 80% got at least one perk, such as a new or extended promotional rate or a price cut ([Consumer Reports, 2018](https://www.consumerreports.org/tv-service/it-pays-to-haggle-over-your-cable-tv-bill)).
- In Consumer Reports' 2017 electronics retailer survey, only 14% of in-store buyers tried to negotiate, but 59% of those got a lower price. Online shoppers who asked succeeded 69% of the time. And 31% of the in-store negotiators did nothing more than ask for a better price ([Consumer Reports, 2017](https://www.consumerreports.org/saving-money/haggling-really-works-when-you-buy-new-tv-laptop-or-other-device/)).
- In a 2023 LendingTree survey of US consumers, 60% of people who had medical debt said they had negotiated their bills, and 90% of them had at least some success ([LendingTree, 2023](https://www.lendingtree.com/debt-consolidation/medical-debt-bills-survey/)).

You're not demanding anything. You're asking, and businesses get these questions every day.

## Before you ask: know your numbers

Confidence comes from facts. Before you reach out, check:

- what you pay now, and how often
- what you actually use
- what competitors or other plans charge, if you can compare
- the outcome you want: a fee waived, a lower rate, a different plan, better terms or an extra included

A grounded opener:

> "I looked at my spending and noticed this cost has become one I want to reduce."

Even a quick look at your spending helps you go into the conversation with a steady voice. You know what you're asking for and why.

## The discount script you can use anywhere

Use this for any company, service provider or recurring bill:

> "Hi, I'm reviewing my spending and I'd like to reduce this cost if possible. I've been a customer for a while, and I wanted to ask what options are available. Is there a discount, promotion, loyalty rate or lower plan you can offer?"

If they ask what you're looking for:

> "Ideally, I'd like the cost reduced or the plan adjusted so it fits my budget better."

To be specific without naming an exact number:

> "I'm looking for a lower monthly cost, a fee waived, or a plan that better matches what I actually use."

That last line keeps the focus on fit, not on struggle. If you've been with a company for years, the dedicated [loyalty discount script](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-ask-for-a-loyalty-discount-simple-script/) shows how to lead with your history.

## Phone script

Phone calls can feel more intense because you have to answer in real time, so keep the opening short.

> "Hi, I'm calling because I'm reviewing my regular expenses and this bill stood out. I'd like to know if there's a lower rate, discount or different plan available."

Then pause. Let them answer. You don't need to fill the silence.

If they say, "Let me check," say:

> "Thank you, I appreciate it."

If they offer something small:

> "Thanks for checking. Is that the best option available, or is there another discount or retention offer you can look at?"

That's polite, and it keeps the conversation open.

## How to ask for a discount in chat

Live chat is often the easiest channel. You can prepare your message ahead of time, paste it in, and take a moment before each reply.

Opening message:

> "Hi, I'm reviewing my expenses and would like to reduce this bill. Can you check whether I'm eligible for a discount, promotional rate, loyalty offer or lower plan?"

If the agent says there are no discounts:

> "Thanks for checking. Are there any upcoming promotions, lower tiers, or fees that can be removed from my account?"

If they still say no:

> "I understand. Can you note on my account that I asked about lowering the cost, and tell me when I'd be eligible to check again?"

A few chat tips:

- **Ask one question per message.** Short messages are easier for agents to act on.
- **Save or screenshot the transcript** if they offer anything, so you have it in writing.
- **Ask for a confirmation** before you close the chat: "Can you confirm the new price and when it starts?"

For shopping chats on retail websites, a short version works:

> "Hi! I'm about to order [item]. Are there any discount codes, current promotions or first-order offers I can use?"

## How to politely ask for a discount in email

Use email when you want a written record, or when the relationship is more personal, such as with a freelancer, supplier, course provider, landlord or local business.

**Related:** [How to Ask Your Landlord for a Repair Reimbursement](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-ask-your-landlord-for-a-repair-reimbursement/)

**Subject line options:**

> Request to review pricing  
> Question about available discounts  
> Request for a lower rate or adjusted plan

**Polite email script:**

> Hi [name],
>
> I'm reviewing my expenses and wanted to ask whether there is any flexibility on the current price.
>
> I value the service, but I'm trying to bring this cost down if possible. Is there a discount, adjusted plan, fee waiver or alternative option available?
>
> Thank you for checking,  
> [your name]

For a slightly warmer tone, add:

> "I'd like to continue if we can find a price or plan that works better."

That's clear without sounding threatening.

## How to ask for a discount professionally (quotes, suppliers and services)

In business or freelance situations, tie the request to something concrete: volume, commitment, timing or scope. That makes it a trade, not a favor.

**Professional email script:**

> Hi [name],
>
> Thank you for the quote for [project/service]. We'd like to move forward, and I wanted to ask whether there's any flexibility on pricing.
>
> For example, would a lower rate be possible if we [commit to a longer term / pay upfront / order a larger quantity / adjust the scope to X]?
>
> Happy to discuss what works on your side.
>
> Best regards,  
> [your name]

Things you can offer in exchange:

- paying upfront or on a faster schedule
- a longer contract or a repeat order
- flexible timing, such as booking in a slower season
- a smaller scope or fewer extras

If you're getting quotes for work on your home, there's a separate script for [asking a contractor for a lower quote](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-ask-a-contractor-for-a-lower-quote-simple-script/).

## In-person script

In a shop or at a local business, keep it light and friendly:

> "I really like this. Is there any discount available today, or any upcoming sales I should know about?"

Other openings that work well:

> "Is there any flexibility on the price if I buy two?"  
> "This one has a small mark. Could you take something off?"  
> "Do you offer any student, senior or first-time customer discounts?"

Ask the person who can actually decide. If the staff member can't, it's fine to ask, "Is there a manager who could check?"

## If they say X, you say Y

**"We don't offer discounts."**

> "I understand. Are there any lower plans, loyalty options, seasonal promotions or fees that can be removed?"

**"This is already our best price."**

> "Thanks for confirming. If the price can't change, is there anything else you can adjust, such as the plan, features, billing terms or fees?"

**"You'd need to cancel to get a better offer."**

> "I'd prefer not to cancel if there's a reasonable option available. Could you check whether there are any retention offers on my account?"

**"Why do you need a discount?"**

> "I'm reviewing my spending and this cost no longer fits as well as it used to. I'm looking for a more suitable option."

You don't need to share personal details unless you want to.

## What not to say

- **Don't apologize repeatedly.** One "thanks for checking" is enough.
- **Don't bluff.** Only mention a competitor or cancelling if you'd really do it.
- **Don't be rude to the agent.** They often decide how hard to look for an offer.
- **Don't accept the first "no" as final** if the service is easy to replace. Ask once more, politely.

## What to do if the first try doesn't work

The first person you talk to may not be allowed to help. That doesn't mean the answer is final. Try:

> "Is there someone else who can review the available options?"

> "Can you transfer me to the retention or customer care team?"

> "When would be a better time to check again for promotions or plan changes?"

If you could replace the service, you can say so calmly:

> "I'm comparing options right now, and I'd prefer to stay if we can make the cost work better."

That isn't a threat. It's useful context.

The same calm approach works for related requests too, such as [asking your bank to waive a fee](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-ask-for-a-bank-fee-waiver-simple-script/) or [asking for a partial refund](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-ask-for-a-partial-refund-without-awkwardness/) when something didn't meet expectations.

## A calm way to close the conversation

If they help you:

> "Thank you, I appreciate you checking that. Can you confirm when the change takes effect?"

If they can't help:

> "Thanks for checking. I'll review my options and decide what makes sense."

That's enough.

Asking for a discount isn't about being difficult. It's about having a say in the price you pay. You can be kind, professional and still ask for a better deal.
