Here's the short answer. For most people, a graduation gift of **$25 to $100** is generous and appropriate. Close family, like parents, grandparents and godparents, often give more. Friends, neighbors and coworkers can give less, and nobody will think less of them for it. Your relationship to the graduate counts for more than the exact dollar amount, and so does what you can comfortably afford.

Let's go through how to choose your number.

## What people actually spend

The best US data comes from the National Retail Federation (NRF). In its 2026 survey, **39% of US consumers planned to buy a gift for a high school or college graduate**, and total spending was expected to reach a record **$7.2 billion**. Cash was again the top gift people planned to give ([NRF](https://www.nrf.com/research-insights/holiday-data-and-trends/graduation)).

NRF's public pages don't list the average amount per buyer for 2026. In the 2025 survey, the average US gift buyer planned to spend about **$119.54** ([NGPF, citing NRF](https://dev4.ngpf.org/blog/question-of-the-day/question-of-the-day-how-much-does-the-average-american-plan-to-spend-on-a-graduation-gift/)). That figure covers everything the person bought for graduates, sometimes for more than one grad, so it isn't the right amount for one gift.

**Bottom line:** if you spend well under $100 on a single graduate, you're still in a normal range.

## Spending ranges by relationship

These ranges are general guidelines, not survey results. Adjust them for your budget, your family's traditions and what's normal where you live.

| Your relationship to the graduate | Typical range (USD) |
|---|---|
| Parent or guardian | $100–$500+, or a meaningful item or experience |
| Grandparent | $50–$250 |
| Aunt, uncle or godparent | $50–$150 |
| Close family friend | $25–$100 |
| Friend or classmate | $10–$40 |
| Neighbor, coworker's child or acquaintance | $20–$50 |
| Graduate you're not close to (you only got an announcement) | A card, or $10–$25 |

Close relatives are at the top of the table because they're usually the ones helping with a big transition, like moving into a dorm, starting a first job or setting up a first apartment. Classmates and acquaintances are at the bottom because a thoughtful card is often enough. For gifts between students in other situations, see [how much to spend on a classmate's birthday gift](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-much-should-you-spend-on-a-classmates-birthday-gift/).

## Adjust for the milestone

Not every graduation is the same size. Here's a rough guide:

- **Preschool, kindergarten, elementary or middle school:** small and fun. Think $10–$25, a book or a small toy.
- **High school:** the classic graduation gift. The relationship ranges in the table above apply here.
- **College (bachelor's degree):** often similar to high school, or a little more from close family, since many grads are about to start working or moving.
- **Graduate or professional degree (master's, law, medical):** these are often smaller, more personal gifts. Many recipients are already working, so something useful for their career can mean more than a large check.

Here's a hypothetical example. Picture an aunt with two graduates this year: a niece finishing high school and a nephew finishing a master's degree. She might give her niece $75 toward college supplies and her nephew a $40 professional notebook with a handwritten note. Neither gift is "worth" more than the other. Each one fits where that graduate is in life.

## Cash, gift cards or a physical gift?

Each option has its strengths:

- **Cash or a money transfer:** Flexible and always welcome, which is probably why it tops NRF's list. You can make it feel more personal with a note about what you hope they'll use it for.
- **Gift cards:** Good if you know where they shop. Some cards can go unused, so pick a store or service the graduate actually uses. If you get cards yourself, here's [how to track gift cards and store credits](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-track-gift-cards-and-store-credits-without-losing-money/) so they don't go to waste.
- **Practical items:** Bedding, kitchen basics, a good backpack or a work bag. These help most for grads moving out. Ask a parent what's still on the list.
- **Keepsakes:** A framed photo, a letter or a family item. These cost little or nothing and are often remembered longest.

## When you have several graduates in one season

Graduation season can bring a lot of invitations at once. Here's how to keep it manageable:

1. **Add up your graduates first.** List everyone you expect to give to before you buy anything.
2. **Set one season total.** For example, if you can comfortably spend $200 and have four grads, that's about $50 each. You can adjust up or down by relationship.
3. **Tier your list.** Close family gets the larger share, and acquaintances get a card or a smaller amount.
4. **Plan ahead next time.** Graduations are predictable. You can set aside a small amount each month in a [yearly gift fund](https://monee-app.com/blog/birthday-budget-set-a-yearly-gift-fund-without-guessing/) that covers birthdays, graduations and holidays.

Around the same time, you may also be buying end-of-year thank-you gifts. Our guide on [how much to spend on a teacher gift](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-much-should-you-spend-on-a-teacher-gift/) can help you budget for those.

## Do you have to bring a gift to a graduation party?

Usually a gift is customary if you attend the party, but it doesn't have to be expensive. If you were only sent an announcement and can't attend, a card with a few heartfelt lines is a polite and fully acceptable response. Families send announcements to share news, not to ask for gifts.

If money is tight, it's fine to give less, or to give your time instead. You could offer to help with a move or review a résumé.

## A note on very large gifts (US)

Most graduation gifts never come near any tax limit, but it helps to know the rules if a relative is planning something big. In the US, the IRS annual gift tax exclusion for 2026 is **$19,000 per recipient**. Married couples who give jointly can give **$38,000** ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/frequently-asked-questions-on-gift-taxes)). The IRS also treats tuition you pay for someone as a separate exclusion from the annual one. Rules on how the payment has to be made apply, so check IRS Publication 559 or ask a tax professional before giving a large gift toward school costs. Other countries have different rules.

## Quick recap

- **Typical range:** $25–$100 for most people, and more from close family.
- **Relationship counts most:** the closer you are, the larger or more personal the gift.
- **Match the milestone:** small for early grades, standard for high school and college, personal for advanced degrees.
- **Budget for the season:** if you have several grads, set one total and split it.
- **A sincere card always counts:** no graduate will remember the exact dollar amount, but they may remember your words.
