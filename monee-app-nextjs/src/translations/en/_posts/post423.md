The short answer: **borrow books you expect to read once, and buy books you expect to reread, reference, annotate, or keep for a long time**.

That rule is useful, but it misses something important: borrowing is not always free in practice, and buying is not always expensive after you account for repeated use or resale. A cost-per-read test gives you a more personal answer.

## What Is Cost per Read?

Cost per read measures how much a book costs each time you use it.

For a purchased book:

> **Cost per read = (purchase price + extra costs − resale value) ÷ expected number of reads**

Extra costs might include shipping, travel to collect the book, or interest if the purchase adds to credit-card debt.

For a borrowed book:

> **Cost per read = (borrowing fees + travel costs + expected penalties) ÷ completed reads**

Many public libraries offer free membership to eligible local residents, but eligibility, replacement charges, loan periods, and other rules vary. Check your library’s current terms rather than assuming every service is free.

Digital borrowing may remove travel costs. Services such as Libby also return borrowed titles automatically at the end of the lending period, so those loans do not generate overdue fines. Availability still depends on whether your library offers the title and has a copy ready ([Libby Help](https://help.libbyapp.com/en-us/6008.htm)).

## A Simple Cost-per-Read Example

Consider a hypothetical paperback that costs $18.

You expect to read it once and sell it for $5:

> ($18 − $5) ÷ 1 = **$13 per read**

Suppose borrowing it involves two trips costing $3 each:

> $6 ÷ 1 = **$6 per read**

Borrowing is cheaper in this example.

But imagine that you expect to read the book four times and keep it:

> $18 ÷ 4 = **$4.50 per read**

Buying now has the lower cost per read.

These figures are illustrative. Use your actual local prices, transport costs, library terms, and realistic reading plans.

## When Borrowing Usually Makes More Sense

Borrowing tends to work well when:

- You are unsure whether you will finish the book.
- You expect to read it only once.
- You are exploring a new author, genre, or subject.
- The book is expensive relative to your reading budget.
- You have limited storage space.
- A library copy is available without a long or inconvenient wait.
- You do not need permanent access or extensive annotations.

Borrowing can also reduce the cost of abandoned books. If a title does not suit you, returning it is usually easier than arranging a sale.

Ask yourself: **Am I paying for the book, or for the chance to find out whether I like it?** If it is mainly the second, borrowing may be the better first step.

## When Buying Usually Makes More Sense

Buying may offer better value when:

- You expect to reread the book.
- You will use it regularly for work, study, cooking, repair, or another practical purpose.
- You want to highlight or write notes in it.
- You need immediate and reliable access.
- The library does not carry it or has a long waiting list.
- The purchase price is low, especially for a used copy.
- The book has meaningful resale value.
- Keeping a personal collection matters to you.

A reference book may deliver value through dozens of short consultations rather than complete cover-to-cover readings. In that case, “cost per use” is more accurate than “cost per read.”

## Do Not Ignore the Cost of Waiting

Borrowing can have a time cost even when it has no cash cost.

A long wait may matter if the book is needed for a course, project, book club, or time-sensitive decision. It may matter much less when the book is simply one option in a long reading list.

You can estimate the value of faster access without pretending that every hour has a precise price. Try asking:

- Will waiting prevent progress on something important?
- Is another suitable book available now?
- Am I likely to lose interest before the loan becomes available?
- Would buying create financial stress?

Convenience has value, but its value depends on the situation.

## Include the Books You Do Not Finish

Expected reads should reflect your real habits, not your best intentions.

Suppose you buy five $20 books but finish only three. Ignoring resale value, you spent $100 for three completed reads:

> $100 ÷ 3 = **$33.33 per completed read**

This does not mean the unfinished books were worthless. Browsing, reference use, or discovering that a book is not right for you may still have value. However, including abandoned purchases prevents the calculation from making ownership look cheaper than it is.

If you often stop reading books early, a “borrow first, buy later” approach can work well.

## Print, E-book, or Audiobook?

Format changes both cost and usefulness.

A print book may be easier to lend, resell, display, or annotate. An e-book can be convenient to carry and search, but access and transfer rights depend on the seller’s terms and digital-rights restrictions. An audiobook may cost more than a paperback while providing more value during travel, exercise, or household tasks.

Compare formats using the same questions:

- What is the total price?
- How often will you use it?
- Can you resell, share, or transfer it?
- Does the format make completion more likely?
- Is the preferred format available through your library?

The cheapest format is not automatically the best value if you are unlikely to use it.

## Try a Two-Stage Decision

You do not always have to choose permanently between borrowing and buying.

A practical sequence is:

1. Borrow the book first.
2. Read enough to judge its long-term usefulness.
3. Buy it only if you want to reread, annotate, reference, or keep it.

This approach is especially useful for nonfiction, cookbooks, professional books, and expensive editions. It separates the cost of evaluation from the cost of ownership.

Another option is to buy used and resell afterward. Your true cost is then the purchase price plus selling expenses minus the amount recovered.

## A Quick Buy-or-Borrow Test

Before getting your next book, estimate:

| Question | Borrowing is favored when… | Buying is favored when… |
|---|---|---|
| How many times will you use it? | Once or possibly not at all | Several times |
| How soon do you need it? | Waiting is acceptable | Immediate access matters |
| Is a copy available? | It is available conveniently | It is unavailable or heavily reserved |
| Will you annotate it? | No | Yes |
| Does it have resale value? | Little relevance | You can recover part of the price |
| Is storage limited? | Yes | No |
| Does ownership matter to you? | Not particularly | Keeping it adds real value |

Then calculate both options with honest estimates.

## The Best Answer Depends on What You Value

Cost per read is a decision aid, not a rule that says every book must justify itself financially. A beautiful edition, a signed copy, or a shelf of favorite novels can provide value beyond reading alone. That value is personal, but it should still fit comfortably within your budget.

For routine decisions, the pattern is straightforward: borrow uncertain or one-time reads, then buy proven favorites and frequently used references. The better choice is the one that matches how you actually read—not how you hope you will read someday.
