You took out $100, and three days later you have $23 and can't remember where the rest went. That's normal. Cash is easy to spend and easy to forget, because nothing sends you a notification when you hand it over.

**The quick fix:** count the cash you have, subtract it from the amount you started with, and log the difference. Rebuild whatever purchases you can remember, and put the rest under one honest line such as "cash, unaccounted." Then set up a small habit so the gap shrinks next time.

Here's how to do each step without stress.

## Why is cash so easy to forget?

A card leaves a record you can check later. Cash doesn't. The only record is whatever you write down.

Cash also tends to go on small, quick purchases: coffee, parking, a snack, a tip. Each one feels too small to bother logging, but together they add up. Cash still matters in the US. Pew Research Center, using Federal Reserve data, reports that cash made up [14% of US consumer payments in 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/09/30/from-cash-to-credit-how-americans-payment-methods-have-changed-in-the-past-decade/), and the average cash purchase was $58. That isn't spare change.

So if you keep forgetting, it doesn't mean you're bad with money. Cash has no built-in reminder, so you'll need to add one yourself.

## Step 1: Do a "cash count" catch-up

This is the method to use when you've already lost track.

1. **Find your starting number.** Check your bank app for the ATM withdrawal or cash-back amount. Add any cash you got as change, gifts, or repayments from friends.
2. **Count what's left.** Check your wallet, pockets, car, and bag.
3. **Subtract.** Starting cash minus remaining cash equals what you spent.
4. **Log that total.** Even if you can't break it down yet, the full amount is in your records now.

**Hypothetical example:** You withdrew $100 on Friday, and on Monday you have $23. That's $77 spent. You remember $12 for lunch and $20 for a haircut tip, so you log those two. The remaining $45 goes under "cash, unaccounted." Your total is still correct.

Don't skip the unaccounted line. A rough record that adds up to the right total is better than a neat one that leaves $45 out.

## Step 2: Jog your memory (without obsessing)

Before you give up on the unaccounted amount, spend about five minutes on these clues:

- **Your calendar:** Where were you each day?
- **Your camera roll:** Photos often show the farmers' market, the food truck, or the bar.
- **Your messages:** Did you split a bill, pay someone back, or chip in for a gift?
- **Your pockets and bag:** Crumpled receipts are surprisingly common.
- **Your routine:** Laundry, vending machines, parking meters, and tips are frequent culprits.

Once the five minutes are up, stop. If you can't trace $10, it's fine to leave it unaccounted. The goal is to see the overall pattern, not to have perfect records.

## Step 3: Pick a system that fits how you actually behave

Which method sticks depends on your habits, so here are a few options.

### Log the withdrawal as "spent"

When you take out cash, log the whole amount at once under a "cash" category. You're treating it as spent the moment it leaves your account. This is the lowest-effort method. You won't see details, but your totals are always right.

### Give cash its own "account"

Keep a separate cash balance in your tracker. Add withdrawals to it, take purchases out of it, and compare it with your wallet once a week. If the two numbers don't match, you know exactly how much needs a catch-up.

### Use envelopes

Putting cash into labeled envelopes (groceries, fun, gas) means the envelope is your record: when it's empty, the category is done. ### Photograph receipts

If you always have your phone in your hand, take a photo of the receipt at the register. Then log a batch of them later, for example on Sunday evening.

## Step 4: Make logging fast enough that you'll actually do it

Most people forget to log because it feels like a chore. Here's how to make it smaller:

- **Tie it to a habit you already have.** Log as soon as you put your wallet away, or as you walk back to your car.
- **Set a daily phone reminder.** A two-minute evening check catches most purchases while you still remember them.
- **Keep categories simple.** "Food," "Transport," "Fun," and "Other" are enough to start with.
- **Use a short note instead of a full description.** "Coffee w/ Sam" is enough.

## Step 5: Do a weekly five-minute check

Once a week:

1. Count your cash.
2. Compare it with your tracker's cash balance.
3. Log any difference.
4. Glance at your unaccounted line. Is it shrinking?

If the unaccounted amount keeps growing, take that as a signal, not a failure. It can mean cash is going to purchases you'd rather not look at closely. Stress and impulse spending often happen in cash for that reason. ## What if you share cash with a partner?

Shared cash is twice as easy to lose track of. Agree on one simple rule, such as "whoever spends it logs it," and do the weekly count together. Keep it light, because the point is to find gaps, not to blame each other. ## Quick recap

- **Already lost track?** Starting cash minus current cash equals spent. Log it now.
- **Can't remember everything?** Rebuild what you can and label the rest "unaccounted."
- **Going forward:** Choose a system: withdrawal-as-spent, a cash account, envelopes, or receipt photos.
- **Make it easy:** Tie logging to a habit you already have and keep categories simple.
- **Check weekly:** Count your cash, compare it with your tracker, and fix the gap.

You don't need a perfect record of every dollar. You need totals that are close enough to tell you where your money really goes, and the cash count gives you that even when you forget to log.
