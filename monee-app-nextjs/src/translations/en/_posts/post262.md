**Short answer: most households should keep enough cash at home to pay for about three days of essentials, mostly in small bills, and stop there.** For many people that works out to a few hundred dollars. If you live somewhere remote, depend on medication, or lose power often, stretching it to about a week of essentials is reasonable. Your emergency fund itself, meaning months of expenses, belongs in an insured bank or credit union account, not under the mattress.

The rules, statistics and insurance figures below are for the **United States** unless stated otherwise.

## The quick verdict

- **Great:** a small stash sized to **three days of essentials**, mostly small bills, stored discreetly.
- **Okay:** up to about **a week of essentials** *if* you have a specific reason, such as living in a rural area, having medical needs, or getting frequent outages.
- **Risky:** "months of expenses" in cash at home "just in case."

You're weighing two real problems:

1. **Payment systems sometimes fail.** Storms, power outages, network problems and local disasters can take down card readers and ATMs.
2. **Your home is not a bank.** Theft, fire, water damage and plain misplacement can wipe out cash, and as shown below, insurance barely covers it.

Ready.gov, the U.S. government's preparedness site, lists **cash** in its family emergency kit and says supplies should last "for at least three days, and possibly longer if you are in a remote or hard-to-reach area." ([Ready.gov: Build a Kit](https://www.ready.gov/kids/family-emergency-planning/build-a-kit)) Its main kit page also lists "cash or traveler's checks" among additional emergency supplies. ([Ready.gov: Build a Kit](https://www.ready.gov/kit)) That three-day baseline is a sensible way to size emergency cash too.

## How to work out your number: "three days of essentials"

Don't pick a round number at random. Add up what you'd actually need to spend if cards and ATMs were unreliable for a few days:

- One grocery run of shelf-stable basics and water
- Fuel or transportation
- Prescriptions, copays or medical essentials
- Pet food, baby supplies, or anything else your household can't skip
- A buffer for one-off outage costs like ice, batteries or a hotel night

**Hypothetical example:** a household estimates $120 for groceries, $60 for fuel, $40 for a prescription copay and $80 for extras. That makes a three-day target of about $300. A household with more people, pets or medical needs will land higher. Treat these numbers as an illustration, not a benchmark.

### Small bills matter more than the total

When the power is out, getting change is hard. A few $100 bills can be close to useless at a gas station running on cash only. Keep mostly $1s, $5s, $10s and $20s, plus a few coins, so you can pay close to exact amounts.

## Why cash still matters even though fewer people use it

Cash use in the U.S. has fallen a lot, but it hasn't disappeared.

- In the Federal Reserve's payment diary, cash made up **14% of consumer payments in 2024**, and consumers averaged **seven cash payments per month**, a number unchanged since 2020. Cash ranked third behind credit cards (35%) and debit cards (30%). ([Federal Reserve Financial Services: 2025 Findings from the Diary of Consumer Payment Choice](https://frbservices.org/news/research/2025-findings-from-the-diary-of-consumer-payment-choice))
- Pew Research Center's analysis of the Atlanta Fed's 2025 survey found the cash share held at **14% in 2025**, down from 33% in 2015. The average cash purchase was **$58**. ([Pew Research Center, Sept. 30, 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/09/30/from-cash-to-credit-how-americans-payment-methods-have-changed-in-the-past-decade/))

So most day-to-day spending now runs on cards and phones. That's exactly why a modest cash stash is useful: it's the backup for when that system goes down.

## The real risk is losing it

Keeping cash at home means you're insuring yourself against outages, and taking on physical risks in return.

### 1) Cash at home isn't FDIC-insured

The FDIC is direct about this: "Cash that's not in a deposit account isn't protected by FDIC insurance." ([FDIC: Safe deposit boxes, home safes, and valuables](https://www.fdic.gov/consumer-resource-center/five-things-know-about-safe-deposit-boxes-home-safes-and-your-valuables))

### 2) Homeowners insurance covers very little cash

Standard U.S. homeowners policies put a low cap on money. Texas A&M's Real Estate Research Center notes that the standard ISO HO-3 form provides "only $200 coverage" for currency and coins, and that Texas's HO-B form has a $100 limit for money. ([Texas A&M Real Estate Research Center](https://trerc.tamu.edu/?p=20463)) Limits differ by insurer, form and state, so check your own policy. Either way, assume that a large cash pile lost in a fire or burglary is mostly gone.

### 3) Fire is a real risk

The U.S. Fire Administration reports that in **2021**, U.S. fire departments responded to an estimated **170,000 home cooking fires**, which caused about **135 deaths**, **3,000 injuries** and more than **$494 million** in property loss. Cooking is the leading cause of home fires. ([USFA: Cooking Fire Safety](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/prevention/home-fires/prevent-fires/cooking/)) These figures aren't specific to cash, but they show that a home is not a vault.

## Should you keep your emergency fund in cash or invest it?

Neither, for the most part. The usual setup has three layers:

1. **Cash at home:** just your three-day (up to one-week) outage stash, as described above.
2. **Insured savings:** the bulk of your emergency fund. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) says an emergency fund should be "safe, accessible, and in a place where you're not tempted to spend it on non-emergencies." It calls bank and credit union accounts "generally considered one of the safest places to put your money," and warns that "cash can be stolen, lost, or destroyed." ([CFPB: An essential guide to building an emergency fund](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund/)) At FDIC-insured banks, deposits are insured to at least **$250,000** per bank. Checking, savings, money market deposit accounts and CDs all qualify. ([FDIC: Deposit Insurance](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance))
3. **Investments:** money you won't need on short notice. The FDIC lists stocks, bonds, mutual funds, annuities and crypto assets as products it does **not** insure. ([FDIC: Deposit Insurance](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance)) Investments can be down just when an emergency hits, so they make a poor first line of defense, though they generally suit long-term goals better than savings do.

A separate savings account just for emergencies, ideally one that earns interest, gives you safety and access without the theft and fire risk of keeping cash at home.

## Who this is for, and who should skip it

**A cash stash makes sense if you:**
- Have been stuck in a multi-day outage, or live where storms regularly knock out power.
- Are paid partly in cash or tips and already handle cash carefully.
- Want a simple backup that works when networks don't.

**Skip it or keep it small if you:**
- Find that cash at home tempts you to overspend ("it's already out, might as well use it").
- Would be using cash at home *instead of* building bank savings.
- Want to hold large amounts because you're anxious about banks, without a specific, realistic scenario in mind.

## How to store emergency cash without creating a new problem

- **Hide it somewhere boring.** Avoid the obvious drawer and labeled envelopes.
- **Consider a fire- and water-resistant safe.** It reduces some risks but doesn't remove them, and it doesn't change the insurance limits.
- **Split it between two places** so one incident doesn't wipe you out, for example most at home and a small amount in your grab-and-go kit.
- **Check it once or twice a year.** Make sure it's still there and still in small bills, and adjust it if your essential costs have changed.
- **Log it when you spend it.** Emergency cash quietly gets used on everyday things. ## FAQ

**Should I keep cash in my emergency kit or just somewhere in the house?**
Ideally both: a small grab-and-go amount in your kit, and the rest stored securely at home.

**Is a safe deposit box safer?**
It's safer from home theft and fire, but you may not be able to get to it during a disruption. Also, the FDIC says a safe deposit box "is not a deposit account," so the cash inside isn't FDIC-insured, and banks generally don't insure box contents either. ([FDIC: Safe deposit boxes, home safes, and valuables](https://www.fdic.gov/consumer-resource-center/five-things-know-about-safe-deposit-boxes-home-safes-and-your-valuables))

**Does my homeowners or renters insurance cover stolen cash?**
Only up to a small limit, often around $200 on standard U.S. forms. Check your policy's "special limits" section. ([Texas A&M Real Estate Research Center](https://trerc.tamu.edu/?p=20463))

**How much should I keep if I live alone?**
Use the same method: add up three days of your own essential costs. One person usually needs less than a family, but medical needs or a long drive to the nearest store can push the number up.

**Do expense trackers solve the "I need emergency cash" problem?**
Not directly. Tracking helps you plan and save. Emergency cash is about being able to pay when systems fail. The two work together. To plan when you'll top up your savings, a [90-day cash flow forecast](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-build-a-90-day-cash-flow-forecast-youll-actually-use/) helps.
