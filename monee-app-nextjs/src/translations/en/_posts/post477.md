For most families, **$10 to $25 per teacher is a reasonable amount**. If you join a class group gift, chipping in **$5 to $10** is normal. Spending nothing and sending a sincere handwritten note is also fine, and many teachers value notes highly.

That's the short answer. Below, the decision is broken into a few simple pieces so you can choose an amount that fits your family's budget.

## What other families tend to spend

There's very little recent, reliable spending data. Here are the most useful figures we could find:

- **In the US**, a Study.com survey found that [roughly 43% of teacher gifts fall in the $10 to $20 range](https://www.wdbj7.com/2025/05/14/early-years-showing-teacher-appreciation).
- **In the UK**, a 2017 Mumsnet poll of more than 1,000 parents found that [the average end-of-year gift for a primary school teacher was £10.60](https://fenews.co.uk/?p=9628). Just over one in ten parents spent £25 or more. The same coverage reported that 44% of parents felt pressured to give an end-of-year present.

Picture the spread like this: most gifts sit in a modest middle range, and a small group spends a lot more. You don't have to match the top of that range. A typical gift is closer to the price of a takeout lunch than a night out.

## Here's how it breaks down by situation

| Situation | Typical range (US) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Individual gift, main classroom teacher | $10–$25 | A gift card plus a note is a popular combination |
| Contribution to a class group gift | $5–$10 per family | Usually adds up to a more useful gift |
| Specialists (music, PE, art), aides, bus drivers | $5–$10 or a note | Small and sincere works well |
| Middle and high school (several teachers) | $5–$10 each, or notes only | Spread a fixed amount across teachers |
| Tight month or tight year | $0 | A specific, handwritten thank-you is enough |

These ranges are general guidance, not rules. Where you live, how your school does things and your own finances all matter more than any chart.

## Check your school's rules first

This is the step people skip. Some teachers are public employees, and gift rules can apply to them.

Take **Massachusetts in the US** as an example. The State Ethics Commission says public employees, including public school teachers, [generally may not accept gifts worth $50 or more](https://www.mass.gov/news/state-ethics-commission-issues-reminder-about-restrictions-on-gifts-to-public-employees-0) given because of their position. Parents may pool money for a class gift worth up to $150 per school year. The gift has to come from the class as a whole, and the teacher can't be told who contributed or how much. The Commission's [Advisory 19-1 on gifts and gratuities](https://www.mass.gov/advisory/19-1-gifts-and-gratuities) has the details.

Rules differ by state, country, district and school. Before spending more than a small amount, look at your school's handbook or ask the front office. If you want to give something bigger, a donation to the classroom is sometimes handled differently from a personal gift.

## What teachers say they actually want

Several teacher surveys point the same way:

- **Gift cards come up often.** In the Study.com survey, [about 45% of teachers favored gift cards](https://www.wdbj7.com/2025/05/14/early-years-showing-teacher-appreciation) because they're flexible.
- **Handwritten notes are loved.** The same report noted that teachers value notes and letters from families.
- **Homemade treats are less popular.** More than a third of teachers in that survey said homemade baked goods were the gift they least wanted.

So a $10 gift card with a few heartfelt lines will usually mean more than a $30 mug or candle. If you give gift cards often, it helps to [track gift cards and store credits](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-track-gift-cards-and-store-credits-without-losing-money/) so leftover balances you receive yourself don't get forgotten.

## Group gifts: often the simplest choice

When a class pools money, each family spends less and the teacher gets one more useful gift. Picture this hypothetical: 20 families each give $5, which makes a $100 gift card. That beats a desk full of small items.

There are a few things to keep in mind:

- **Keep contributions optional and private.** That removes the pressure many parents feel.
- **Suggest an amount, not a minimum.** "Around $5–$10, any amount welcome" works well.
- **Check the total against school rules**, like the Massachusetts class gift limit above.

If you're the one organizing, this guide to [group gifts with one simple split rule](https://monee-app.com/blog/group-gifts-without-stress-one-simple-split-rule/) keeps the money side fair and easy.

## Plan for the whole year, not just one gift

Teacher gifts add up because they come at several points in the year. Here's a simple way to see your total:

**Number of teachers × number of occasions × amount per gift = yearly teacher gift budget**

A hypothetical example: one child, one main teacher, two occasions (winter holidays and end of year), $15 each:

1 × 2 × $15 = **$30 for the year**

Now add a second child, two specialists at $5 each and Teacher Appreciation Week:

- Main teachers: 2 children × 3 occasions × $15 = $90
- Specialists: 2 × 1 occasion × $5 = $10
- **Total: $100 for the year**

Seeing that number ahead of time lets you adjust before you spend. You might drop one occasion, join group gifts instead or switch some gifts to notes. Teacher gifts also fit well inside a broader [yearly gift fund](https://monee-app.com/blog/birthday-budget-set-a-yearly-gift-fund-without-guessing/), alongside things like [classmates' birthday gifts](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-much-should-you-spend-on-a-classmates-birthday-gift/).

## Low-cost and no-cost ideas that still feel thoughtful

If money is tight, these all work:

- **A specific handwritten note.** Mention one moment, lesson or change you noticed in your child.
- **A drawing or letter from your child.**
- **Classroom supplies** from the teacher's wish list, if they have one.
- **A small gift card** ($5 is fine) for coffee or a supply store.
- **An email to the principal** praising the teacher. It costs nothing and can matter to their career.

## The simple takeaway

Here's the whole decision in a few steps:

1. **Check school rules** before spending more than a small amount.
2. **Pick a range:** $10–$25 for an individual gift, $5–$10 toward a group gift, or $0 with a sincere note.
3. **Multiply it out** across teachers and occasions so the yearly total doesn't surprise you.
4. **Choose usefulness over size.** A gift card or classroom supplies plus a few honest words is enough.

A teacher gift is a thank-you, not a contest. Whatever amount fits your budget is the right amount.
