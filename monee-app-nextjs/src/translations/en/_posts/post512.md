Yes, for most bulbs you use every day. An LED that replaces an old incandescent or halogen bulb often pays for itself in a few months. The math changes when you're replacing a CFL or a bulb you hardly ever turn on. In those cases, waiting until the old bulb burns out is usually the smarter choice.

Here's how to test it for your own home, one bulb at a time.

## The payback test in one formula

You need three numbers:

1. **Watts saved**: the old bulb's wattage minus the LED's wattage
2. **Hours per day**: how long the light is usually on
3. **Your electricity rate**: in cents per kilowatt-hour (kWh), from your bill

The math works like this:

- **Yearly savings** = watts saved × hours per day × 365 ÷ 1,000 × your rate
- **Payback in months** = bulb price ÷ yearly savings × 12

If the payback period is shorter than the time you expect to stay in your home, and well within the bulb's lifespan, switching makes sense.

For reference, the average US residential electricity price was **18.19 cents per kWh** for 2026 so far, according to preliminary data from the [US Energy Information Administration (EIA)](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/monthly/epm_table_grapher.php?t=epmt_5_3). Rates vary a lot by state and by country, so use the number on your own bill if you can.

## Why LEDs usually win

The official figures are useful for setting expectations:

- **Energy use:** In the US, [ENERGY STAR](https://energystar.gov/lighting) says certified LED bulbs use up to 90% less energy than standard bulbs and last at least 15 times longer.
- **Lifespan:** ENERGY STAR [puts it in hours](https://www.energystar.gov/products/ask-the-expert/top-5-reasons-why-now-time-rid-your-home-inefficient-light-bulbs): about 1,000 hours for an incandescent bulb, compared with 15,000 hours for a certified LED.
- **Lifetime savings:** ENERGY STAR estimates a certified LED saves about $55 in electricity costs over its life. Your actual figure depends on your rate and how much you use the light.

The lifespan matters as much as the energy savings. A bulb that lasts 15 times longer means fewer replacement bulbs, which the formula above doesn't even count.

## Four hypothetical scenarios

These examples are **hypothetical**. They use the 2026 US average rate of 18.19¢/kWh and assume an LED costs $3. Bulb prices vary, so swap in your own numbers.

All of them use a 9-watt LED, a common replacement for a 60-watt incandescent.

| Scenario | Old bulb | Daily use | Yearly savings | Payback |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Living room lamp | 60W incandescent | 3 hours | about $10.16 | about 3.5 months |
| B. Kitchen light | 43W halogen | 3 hours | about $6.77 | about 5.3 months |
| C. Desk lamp | 13W CFL | 3 hours | about $0.80 | about 3.75 years |
| D. Closet light | 60W incandescent | 10 minutes | about $0.56 | about 5.4 years |

Here's how it breaks down:

**Scenario A** is a clear win. You save 51 watts for 3 hours a day, which is about 56 kWh a year. And because an incandescent lasts about 1,000 hours, at 3 hours a day you'd be buying a new one about once a year anyway.

**Scenario B** is also a quick payback. Halogen bulbs look modern, but they still use far more power than LEDs.

**Scenario C** is where patience pays. CFLs are already fairly efficient, so you only save about 4 watts. Replacing a working CFL takes years to pay off. A sensible approach is to keep it until it dies, then switch.

**Scenario D** shows that usage matters most. A light you barely use doesn't cost much to run, whatever kind of bulb it is. The LED still pays off eventually, but there's no rush.

## A simple order for switching

To keep this manageable, picture your home sorted by how much each light is used:

1. **Switch first:** incandescent and halogen bulbs in rooms you use for hours each day, like the living room, kitchen, porch lights on timers and lamps you read by
2. **Switch when they burn out:** CFLs and bulbs in rooms you use less often
3. **Low priority:** closets, attics and other lights you only switch on for a moment

This way your money goes to the bulbs with the fastest payback first, and you don't throw away bulbs that still work and are already cheap to run. It's the same logic as any [break-even test for reusable products](https://monee-app.com/blog/should-you-switch-to-reusable-products-a-break-even-test/): the more often you use something, the sooner the upfront cost pays off.

## What can change the result

A few details can shift your numbers:

- **Your electricity rate.** A higher rate means a faster payback. EIA's data shows big regional differences within the US.
- **Bulb quality.** A cheap bulb that fails early can wipe out the savings. Look for certification marks such as ENERGY STAR in the US, and keep the receipt in case the bulb comes with a warranty. If an extended protection plan is offered on a $3 bulb, the [extended warranty break-even test](https://monee-app.com/blog/should-you-buy-an-extended-warranty-a-breakeven-test/) will almost always say no.
- **Brightness, not watts.** Compare bulbs by lumens, the measure of light output. That way the new bulb won't be too dim or too harsh.
- **Fixture fit.** If you use a dimmer or an enclosed fixture, check that the LED's packaging says it supports that use.
- **Rebates.** Some US utilities offer lighting discounts or rebates, which shorten the payback even more.

Store-brand LEDs can be fine too, as long as the specs and certification match. The thinking is the same as in the [name brand vs. store brand budget test](https://monee-app.com/blog/name-brand-or-store-brand-a-simple-budget-test/).

## A note on US rules

In the US, the Department of Energy set a minimum efficiency of 45 lumens per watt for most general-service bulbs in a [2022 final rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2022-05-09/html/2022-09477.htm). Full enforcement for retailers began in 2023, according to [Beveridge & Diamond](https://bdlaw.com/publications/u-s-department-of-energy-finalizes-rules-to-impose-stringent-efficiency-standard-on-most-lamps). Most traditional incandescent bulbs can't meet that standard. Proposals to roll back these rules have been introduced, so check the current status if this matters to you. Either way, it mostly affects what you can buy new. It doesn't change the math on the bulbs already in your fixtures.

## The bottom line

Run the test on your most-used lights first. If an old incandescent or halogen bulb is on for a few hours a day, an LED will most likely pay for itself within months. For CFLs and lights you rarely use, the savings are small, so replacing them as they burn out is the calmer and cheaper option.
