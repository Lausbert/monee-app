Yes, you can negotiate with a contractor. Thank them for the quote, say you want to work with them, give the budget you need to stay within, and ask if the price can move or if the scope can change to bring the cost down. Then stop talking and let them answer.

Here is the simplest script:

> "Thank you for sending the quote. I'd really like to work with you, but the total is higher than I expected. Is there any flexibility in the price, or could we adjust the scope to bring the cost down?"

You don't need a long explanation. You're asking a normal business question, not demanding a discount. If asking for money off feels uncomfortable in general, these tips on [how to ask for a discount without feeling awkward](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-ask-for-a-discount-without-feeling-awkward/) apply here too.

## Start With Comparable Written Quotes

A better price is easier to get when you have something to compare against. The US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) advises homeowners to [get multiple estimates](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-avoid-home-improvement-scam). It says a written estimate should describe the work, materials, completion date and price. It also warns: "Don't automatically choose the lowest bidder." If estimates differ a lot, ask why.

**Script to request a quote or project consultation:**

> "Hi [name], I'm planning [project] at [address/area] and would like a written estimate. Could you visit or review photos and give me a quote that lists the scope of work, materials, timeline and total price? Is there any charge for the estimate or consultation?"

Ask about an estimate fee up front, because some contractors charge for site visits or design work.

**Using a platform like Angi or HomeAdvisor (US):** HomeAdvisor's homeowner service now runs under the Angi brand. Angi's [How It Works page](https://www.angi.com/how-it-works) says you can request and compare quotes from local pros or book some services at a set price. To schedule free estimates efficiently:

- Describe the project in detail and add photos so the quotes are comparable.
- Give two or three time windows that suit you for site visits.
- Read reviews and check licensing yourself before you book anyone.
- If you prefer, use the platform to find pros and then call the ones you like directly.

Check the platform's current terms, fees and guarantee details before you book, because they can change.

## What to Check Before You Ask for a Lower Price

Review the quote and work out:

- The total you can realistically afford
- Which parts of the project are essential
- Which features, materials or services could change
- Whether the quote includes optional extras or allowances
- When you need the work finished, and whether you could be flexible on timing

A budget figure gives you a natural opening:

> "I've worked out what I can comfortably put toward this project. I need to keep the total closer to [amount]. Is there a way to adjust the quote?"

You don't have to share personal financial details. A clear budget is enough.

## Phone Script for Asking for a Lower Quote

> "Hi [name], thank you for preparing the quote. I've reviewed everything and I'm happy with the proposed work. The total is above the budget I've set aside, though. Is there any flexibility in the quote, or are there changes we could make to reduce the cost?"

Then pause and let the contractor respond. The silence may feel uncomfortable, but if you fill it you end up negotiating against yourself.

If you have a genuine competing quote, keep the comparison factual:

> "I've received another quote at a lower price, but I feel confident about your approach. Is there any way you could come closer to that price?"

Don't invent a competing offer. Make sure the bids cover the same scope and materials before you compare totals.

## Email Script to Ask for a Better Price on a Quote

**Subject: Question about the quote for [project]**

> Hi [name],
>
> Thank you for sending the quote for [project]. I appreciate the detail you included, and I'm interested in working with you.
>
> The total is currently higher than my planned budget. Is there any flexibility in the price, or could you suggest changes to the scope, materials or timing that would bring the cost down?
>
> I'm hoping to keep the project closer to [amount] while keeping the most important parts of the work.
>
> Thank you,
> [name]

## Short Text or Chat Version

> "Thanks for the quote. I'd like to move forward, but the total is above my budget. Is there any flexibility, or could we change the scope to lower the cost?"

This works well if you've already been talking about the project by text or a messaging app.

## If They Say X, You Say Y

**If they say, "That's already my best price":**

> "I understand. Could you show me which parts of the project we could remove, postpone or simplify to reduce the total?"

**If they ask, "What's your budget?":**

> "I need to keep the project around [amount]. What would be possible within that budget?"

**If they offer cheaper materials:**

> "That could work. How does the alternative compare in durability, appearance and warranty coverage?"

**If they offer a small reduction:**

> "Thank you. If I confirm by [date], is there any further adjustment you could make?"

**If they sound offended:**

> "I respect your work and understand that your pricing reflects your time and experience. I'm just checking whether there's an option that works for both of us."

## Other Ways to Lower the Quote

A lower total doesn't always mean the contractor has to cut their rate. You could ask about:

- Doing the project in stages
- Removing non-essential work
- Choosing different materials or finishes
- Moving the schedule to a time that suits them better
- Doing simple prep or cleanup yourself
- Combining tasks to cut down on repeat visits

## Get the New Price in Writing

Every change you agree should appear in a revised written quote or contract that shows the new scope, materials, timing, payment schedule and total. The FTC ([US](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-avoid-home-improvement-scam)) recommends asking for a written contract even if your state doesn't require one. It says the contract should include:

- The contractor's contact details and license number
- Start and finish dates
- Any verbal promises about scope and costs

It also says to fill in every blank space, not to pay the full amount up front, and not to make the final payment until the work is done and you're satisfied. Some states cap down payments.

If you sign a contract in your home or somewhere other than the seller's permanent place of business, US federal rules generally give you [three business days to cancel](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/buyers-remorse-ftcs-cooling-rule-may-help) under the FTC's Cooling-Off Rule. The rule covers sales of $25 or more at your home and $130 or more at temporary locations. It doesn't cover things like emergency repairs, and state laws may add further protections.

## How to Turn Down a Contractor Quote (Sample)

If you go with someone else or put the project on hold, a short, polite reply keeps the door open:

> "Hi [name], thank you for taking the time to prepare the quote for [project]. I've decided to go in a different direction for now. I appreciated your clear explanation and may reach out again for future work. All the best."

If price was the reason and you'd consider them again:

> "Thank you for the detailed quote. It's above what I can spend right now, so I won't be moving forward at this time. If anything changes on scope or pricing, I'd be glad to hear from you."

## What to Do If the First Try Doesn't Work

If the contractor can't change the price, thank them and take time to decide:

> "Thank you for explaining. I need to review my options before committing. I'll come back to you by [date]."

You can get another quote, shrink the project, delay the work, or decide this contractor's quality is worth the original price. A good conversation doesn't always end with a discount. Sometimes it just makes your choices clearer.
