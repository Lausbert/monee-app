To spend less on gift wrap and cards, stop buying them one at a time at full price. Buy cards in bulk or from discount sources, switch to wrap you can reuse, and count postage as part of the cost. None of these items costs much alone, but they add up over a year of birthdays, holidays, and thank-yous.

You don't have to give up cards or nicely wrapped gifts. The aim is to stop overpaying for things that usually end up in the bin within minutes.

## Why these small costs add up

In the US, the Greeting Card Association has estimated that consumers buy about 6.5 billion greeting cards a year, and most cost between $2 and $4 ([USPS News, citing GCA figures](https://news.usps.com/2019/09/26/facts-on-paper/)). These are 2019 figures, the latest the association has published, so today's shelf prices are often higher.

**Hypothetical example:** say you buy 25 cards a year at $5 each. That's $125. Add a few rolls of wrapping paper, gift bags, tissue, and ribbon, and you could pass $175 before buying a single gift. Cutting card costs to about $1 each and reusing most of your wrap could bring that well under $60.

The savings aren't life-changing. They are easy, though, and you repeat them every year.

## Spend less on greeting cards

### Buy from discount sources

Card prices vary a lot between stores. In June 2026, MoneyLion reported that some US Dollar Tree stores sold Hallmark-made Heartline cards at two for $1 and Expressions by Hallmark cards at $1.50 each ([MoneyLion](https://moneylion.com/trending/money/why-frugal-shoppers-are-choosing-dollar-tree-over-target-for-these-items)). The same report said most cards at Target cost $4.99, with some at $2.99. Prices change by store and over time, so check locally.

The trade-off is choice. Discount stores carry fewer designs, so you won't always find the perfect card at the last minute. That's a good reason to buy ahead.

### Keep a small stock of cards

Most last-minute card purchases happen at full price, at a pharmacy or supermarket checkout. A small box at home fixes that. Fill it with:

- 4–6 general birthday cards (kids and adults)
- 2–3 blank or "thinking of you" cards
- 2–3 thank-you cards
- 1–2 sympathy cards

Blank cards are the most flexible because you write the message yourself. Multipacks of blank or thank-you cards usually cost much less per card than single cards.

### Make or print your own when it makes sense

A folded piece of cardstock with a handwritten note costs very little, and many people value the personal touch more than a printed verse. Children's drawings work well as cards for grandparents, too.

Be realistic, though. If you have to buy craft supplies you'll never use again, homemade cards can cost more than discount ones. They're good value when you already have the materials.

### Use digital cards for the right people

Some people are happy with e-cards or a thoughtful text, especially for casual birthdays. Others, often older relatives, really value a paper card. Save paper cards for those people and go digital for the rest.

## Save on postage for mailed cards

Postage is easy to forget, and in the US it keeps rising. USPS Notice 123 lists $0.82 for a 1-ounce stamped First-Class letter, $0.65 for a postcard, and a $0.49 nonmachinable surcharge for certain letters ([USPS Notice 123](https://pe.usps.com/text/dmm300/Notice123.htm)).

Ways to keep US postage down:

- **Avoid square envelopes and bulky extras.** Square envelopes, along with clasps, ribbons, or raised embellishments, can trigger the nonmachinable surcharge, which raises a single card's postage from $0.82 to $1.31.
- **Watch the weight.** Heavy cardstock, inserts, or photos can push a card over 1 ounce. A 2-ounce letter costs $1.11.
- **Send postcards for casual notes.** At $0.65, a postcard costs less than a letter stamp and needs no envelope.
- **Buy Forever stamps before price increases.** They stay valid for 1-ounce First-Class letters after rates go up. This saves only a little, so it's not worth hoarding large amounts.

## Spend less on gift wrap

### Buy wrap that works year-round

Holiday-themed paper only works in one season. Plain kraft paper, solid colors, or simple patterns work for birthdays, weddings, and holidays alike. One large roll of plain kraft paper often lasts longer than several small decorative rolls.

### Reuse gift bags and boxes

Gift bags are often in good enough shape to use again. Keep a bag of saved gift bags, tissue paper, and ribbon. If you take a little care when opening gifts, much of this material can be reused several times.

### Try fabric wrapping

Fabric wraps, such as the Japanese *furoshiki* method, use a scarf, tea towel, or piece of cloth instead of paper. You can reuse the cloth for years, and it can even be part of the gift. If you have to buy new fabric just for wrapping, the upfront cost is higher than paper, so start with fabric you already own.

### Use what you already have

Plenty of household materials make good wrapping:

- Brown paper bags or packing paper from deliveries
- Newspaper, old maps, or sheet music
- Children's artwork on large paper
- Jars, tins, or baskets for food gifts

Plain paper with twine and a sprig of greenery looks deliberate rather than cheap.

### Skip glitter and foil

Glitter and metallic wraps cost more and are harder to dispose of. In the UK, councils such as West Sussex say foil and metallic paper with a plastic feel, and paper covered in glitter, can't go in household recycling. They suggest a "scrunch test": paper that stays scrunched can usually be recycled ([West Sussex County Council](https://www.westsussex.gov.uk/land-waste-and-housing/waste-and-recycling/recycling-and-waste-prevention-in-west-sussex/a-to-z-of-recycling/greeting-cards-and-wrapping/put-in-your-general-waste-bin)). Recycling rules depend on where you live, so check your local guidance.

## Buy at the right time

- **After major holidays:** Wrapping paper, gift bags, ribbon, and boxed cards are often marked down after the season ends. This only saves money if you'll actually use them next year and have room to store them.
- **Buy plain items any time:** Kraft paper, tissue, and blank cards don't go out of style, so you can buy them whenever they're on sale.
- **Don't overbuy:** A cheap stockpile you never use is still money spent. Buy roughly what you used last year.

## Include cards and wrap in your gift budget

Cards and wrap often aren't part of anyone's gift budget, which is why they slip through. If you already plan a [yearly birthday gift fund](https://monee-app.com/blog/birthday-budget-set-a-yearly-gift-fund-without-guessing/), add a line for cards, wrap, and postage. For each gift, count the card and wrap in the total. For example, if you've worked out [how much to spend on a teacher gift](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-much-should-you-spend-on-a-teacher-gift/) or [a classmate's birthday gift](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-much-should-you-spend-on-a-classmates-birthday-gift/), a $5 card and a $4 gift bag can take a big share of a small budget.

A simple rule: **gift extras shouldn't cost more than about 10–20% of the gift itself.** For a $15 gift, keep the card and wrap to $1.50–$3. This is a guideline, not a hard rule. A meaningful card can be worth more to the person receiving it.

## What's worth saving on, and what isn't

**Worth cutting:**
- Single cards bought at full price at the last minute
- Holiday-only wrapping paper
- Gift bags for gifts you could easily wrap or present bare
- Extra postage caused by square or heavy cards

**Worth keeping:**
- A handwritten message. It costs nothing and is often the part people remember.
- A real card for people who value one, such as older relatives, people who are grieving, or milestone events.
- Decent wrap for occasions where presentation matters to you

## Quick checklist

- Keep a small box of discount and blank cards
- Buy plain, all-occasion wrap instead of seasonal designs
- Save and reuse gift bags, tissue, and ribbon
- Use fabric or household materials where they fit
- Avoid square envelopes and heavy add-ons for mailed cards
- Use digital cards for people who don't mind
- Add cards, wrap, and postage to your yearly gift budget

Gift wrap and greeting cards are small costs, so cutting them is mostly about habits. Buy ahead, reuse what you can, and spend the money where it matters most to the people you're giving to.
