Until a refund actually reaches your account, budget as if it doesn't exist. Count it as money that's on its way, not money you can spend. Plan your next few weeks around what you have now, and give the refund a job only after it arrives.

That can feel frustrating when the money is already yours. But a refund can arrive late, arrive smaller than expected, or need a follow-up. A budget that doesn't depend on it gives you more room to handle any of those outcomes calmly.

Below is a simple way to work through the wait. Some questions along the way may help you figure out what matters most in your situation.

## First: What kind of refund are you waiting for?

The type of refund changes the timeline and what you can do about it. Here are a few common cases. The rules differ by country, so check the ones that apply to you.

**A tax refund (in the US).** The IRS says e-filed returns are usually processed within about 3 weeks, and mailed paper returns take 6 weeks or more after the IRS receives them ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/refunds)). Some refunds take longer. In the US, refunds on returns that claim the Earned Income Tax Credit are held by law until mid-February. Errors, amended returns, and certain other credits can also slow things down ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/refunds)). The IRS "Where's My Refund?" tool updates once a day, usually overnight ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/node/76036)), so checking several times a day won't show you anything new.

**A store or online purchase refund.** The timing depends on the merchant, the payment method, and your local consumer law. In the UK, for example, if you cancel most online purchases within 14 days of delivery, the seller must refund you within 14 days of getting the goods back. The seller is allowed to wait until the item is returned before paying ([Citizens Advice](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/about-us/media-centre/press-releases/refund-time-limit-will-help-consumers-get-their-money-back/)).

**A credit card charge you're disputing (in the US).** Under US rules, you generally need to send a written dispute within 60 days of the statement that first showed the charge. The card issuer must acknowledge your dispute within 30 days and resolve it within two billing cycles, and never more than 90 days ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-long-can-the-card-issuer-take-to-resolve-my-billing-error-or-dispute-en-64)).

**Money a friend or family member owes you.** No official timeline applies here, so it's even more important not to count on the money. If it's starting to feel awkward, these ideas on [what to say when a friend hasn't paid you back yet](https://monee-app.com/blog/what-to-say-when-a-friend-hasnt-paid-you-back-yet/) may help.

Ask yourself: is there a realistic date you can expect this money by, or is it open-ended? Your answer affects how careful your plan needs to be.

## Build a "refund-free" budget for the gap

Start with what's certain:

1. **List the money you'll definitely have** between now and your next paycheck or income date.
2. **List the bills that are due** in that window, such as rent, utilities, minimum debt payments, and insurance.
3. **Add your basic living costs**: groceries, transport, medication, childcare.
4. **Subtract.** What's left is your flexible money for this stretch. Don't count the refund.

If the numbers work, good. The refund becomes a bonus when it arrives instead of a rescue you're anxiously waiting for.

If they don't work, the next sections can help.

## When the gap is tight: Protect essentials first

The order of what to cover can vary depending on your situation. A common approach is:

- **Housing and utilities** first, since falling behind often brings fees or bigger problems.
- **Food, transport to work, and medication** next.
- **Minimum payments** on debts, to avoid late fees.
- **Everything else**, like subscriptions, eating out, and non-urgent purchases, can usually wait a few weeks.

Some questions worth thinking about:

- Which costs could you pause, shrink, or move for a few weeks without real harm?
- Is there a bill you could ask to pay a little later? Some providers will agree if you ask before the due date.
- Is the refund tied to a specific cost, such as a returned item you need to replace? If so, can you wait to buy the replacement until the refund arrives?

**A hypothetical example:** Say you're expecting a $600 tax refund and a $90 refund from an online return. You'd planned to use the tax refund for a car repair. In a refund-free budget, you'd ask: can the repair wait three weeks? If yes, schedule it after the money lands. If not, you might cover part of it now and cut other spending until the refund arrives, rather than borrowing against money you haven't received.

## Be careful about spending money you don't have yet

Waiting can make it tempting to put things on a credit card or use buy now, pay later "because the refund is coming." That can work out if the refund arrives on time and for the full amount. If it doesn't, you could be left with a payment due and nothing to cover it.

If you're thinking about this, compare the refund's likely arrival date with the payment due date. This [refund-risk and due-date checklist for buy now, pay later](https://monee-app.com/blog/should-you-use-buy-now-pay-later-for-travel-a-refundrisk-duedate-checklist/) is written for travel, but the same logic applies to other purchases.

## Keep the refund moving on your side

While you wait, a few small steps can stop delays from building up:

- **Write down what you're owed**: the amount, who owes it, the date you requested it, any reference number, and the expected timeline. This makes follow-up much easier.
- **Check whether anything is waiting on you.** Have you shipped the return? Did the IRS or the seller ask you for information? The IRS contacts taxpayers by mail when it needs more information ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/node/76036)).
- **Follow up once the expected window has passed.** If you're not sure how to word it, this [calm 3-step script for requesting a refund](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-request-a-refund-a-calm-3-step-script-that-works/) works for follow-ups too.
- **Watch the deadlines.** Return windows and dispute windows can close while you're still waiting for a reply. It helps to [track return deadlines so you never lose a refund](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-track-return-deadlines-so-you-never-lose-a-refund/).

## Decide now what the refund is for

Deciding ahead of time can help you avoid spending the refund on whatever happens to be in front of you that day. Some options:

- **Refill what you used.** If you dipped into savings to cover the gap, putting that money back may be the most important job.
- **Catch up on anything you postponed**, such as the bill you moved or the repair you delayed.
- **Pay down debt**, especially debt with high interest.
- **Build a small buffer** so the next wait feels less stressful.
- **Spend some of it on purpose.** Enjoying part of a refund is fine if the essentials are covered.

There's no single right split. What would make the next month feel easier for you: more breathing room, less debt, or crossing something off a list?

## If the refund comes back smaller, or not at all

Sometimes the amount is different from what you expected. A tax refund can be adjusted, or a seller may deduct for use or return shipping where local rules allow it. Because your budget never relied on the refund, a smaller amount is disappointing but doesn't throw everything off.

If you think the amount is wrong, ask for the reason in writing. Then decide whether it's worth pushing back. For purchases, you may also have options short of a full refund, like [asking for a partial refund](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-ask-for-a-partial-refund-without-awkwardness/). If a US card dispute is handled badly, you can file a complaint with the CFPB ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-long-can-the-card-issuer-take-to-resolve-my-billing-error-or-dispute-en-64)).

## In short

- Budget as if the refund is zero until it shows up in your account.
- Find out the realistic timeline for your type of refund and where you live.
- Cover essentials first and delay what can wait.
- Avoid taking on payments that only work if the refund arrives on time.
- Record the details, follow up after the expected window, and watch the deadlines.
- Decide ahead of time what the money will do once it arrives.
