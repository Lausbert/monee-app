Most people spend **$30 to $100** on a baby shower gift. In the US, **about $50** is a typical amount. Give less to a coworker or acquaintance, more to a close friend, and the most to close family. Only go as high as you can comfortably afford.

The $50 figure is the median from a [2023 survey of 1,000 people by Ally](https://ally.com/stories/spend/how-much-should-you-spend-on-a-gift-for-any-occasion), a US bank. In that survey, guests who gave more than most (the top quarter) spent around $75. Ally rounded all its figures up to the nearest $5. We found no newer independent survey of baby shower gift spending for 2025 or 2026, so the 2023 numbers are still the best published benchmark.

## How much to spend by relationship

Ally's survey broke down typical (median) baby shower gift amounts by relationship. These are US figures:

| Your relationship | Typical amount (Ally, US) |
|---|---|
| Acquaintance or coworker | About $30 |
| Close friend | About $50 |
| Family member | About $100 |

These numbers describe what people actually spend. They aren't a rule.

**Outside the US:** A small survey of 100 people by [UK baby brand Baa Baby](https://www.baababy.co.uk/blogs/main-blog/baby-showers-gift-survey) found that 51.7% usually spend £25 to £50 on a baby shower gift. Another 46.5% spend under £25, and just 1% spend more than £100. The survey isn't dated and the sample is small, so treat it as a rough guide. In other countries, use the table to see how amounts scale with closeness, then adjust for local prices and customs.

## How much to spend on a coworker's baby shower gift

If you're buying your own gift for a coworker, **around $20 to $30** is normal. That matches Ally's acquaintance figure of about $30.

Many offices collect money for one group gift instead. Cheddar Up, a US group-payment platform, suggests contributing **[$18 to $35 for a baby shower](https://www.cheddarup.com/blog/how-much-to-contribute-to-a-group-gift-at-work/)**, based on its own collection data. It calls these "merely guidelines." It also notes that people on small teams often chip in more, while people in large offices can give less. If money is tight, it suggests giving a smaller amount along with a heartfelt note.

A few practical points for workplace showers:

- **Chipping in to the group gift is usually enough.** You don't also need to buy a separate gift unless you're close to the coworker.
- **Ask the organizer** what others are giving if you're unsure.
- **Check your employer's gift policy** if there's one.

## How much to spend on a baby sprinkle gift

A sprinkle is a smaller shower for a second or later baby. The [Emily Post Institute](https://emilypost.com/advice/baby-shower-top-etiquette-questions) says these are perfectly fine to hold. It adds that the guest list is usually just immediate family and very close friends, and that the family "likely already has many of the items needed (though not always!)."

No survey gives a sprinkle-specific amount. Because the parents have less to buy, many guests give a smaller gift than they would for a first shower. Something in the **$20 to $50** range works well, such as diapers, wipes, bottles or a small item for the new baby. If you're close family, it's still fine to give more. Check whether the parents have registered for anything new.

## Gift, gift card or cash?

Ally's survey also asked what recipients prefer. For baby showers, **55% wanted a gift, 30% a gift card and 15% cash.** That was the strongest preference for a physical present of any occasion Ally covered. A registry item or a thoughtful physical gift is the safest choice. A gift card in the same amount is a good second option.

## Etiquette doesn't set a price

There's no "correct" amount. The [Emily Post Institute's guidance on baby shower gifts](https://emilypost.com/advice/baby-shower-gifts-and-registries) is a leading US etiquette authority, and it doesn't give a dollar figure. It does say that "gift registries are not marching orders." No guest has to buy from the list.

So the real question is a gentler one: what feels generous to you without straining your budget?

## How to pick your number

These questions can help you settle on an amount quickly.

**How close are you?** A cousin you text every week and a coworker you've met twice are very different. It's normal to give more to the people closest to you.

**What else is on your plate this month?** If several gifts are due at once, a smaller gift is completely fine. You might also have a [teacher gift](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-much-should-you-spend-on-a-teacher-gift/) or a [classmate's birthday present](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-much-should-you-spend-on-a-classmates-birthday-gift/) coming up.

**Will you give again later?** Many people give a second gift when the baby arrives, or for the first birthday. If you plan to, you can split your budget across both.

**Are you invited to more than one shower?** You don't need a full gift for each. Emily Post's advice on [multiple showers](https://emilypost.com/advice/shower-etiquette) is written for bridal showers, but it applies here too. You "only need to bring a gift to the first one." If arriving empty-handed feels awkward, it suggests bringing "something inexpensive, such as a small bouquet, chocolates, or even some homemade goodies."

## Ways to give well on a smaller budget

A modest budget can still buy a thoughtful gift. A few ideas:

- **Go in on a group gift.** Pooling money for a bigger registry item, like a stroller or car seat, is common. The [Emily Post Institute](https://emilypost.com/advice/baby-shower-gifts-and-registries) mentions this too. Your $25 can become part of something the parents really need.
- **Pick the practical stuff.** Diapers, wipes and basic baby care items get used every day. Parents rarely have too many.
- **Make something.** Emily Post says homemade gifts like hand-knit blankets can be treasured for years.
- **Add a personal touch.** A heartfelt card or an offer of help, like dropping off a meal after the birth, can mean as much as the gift itself.

If the parents are deciding what to buy and what to borrow, this guide to [renting versus buying baby gear](https://monee-app.com/blog/should-you-rent-or-buy-baby-gear-a-use-time-test/) can help you pick a gift that won't sit unused.

## Registry or not?

Buying from the registry is the easiest choice. The parents get something they picked, and you avoid duplicates. Emily Post also notes that registered items are more likely to meet current child-safety standards. For safety gear like car seats and cribs, stick to the registry.

Going off the registry is fine too, especially for a handmade item or a "signature" gift you like to give.

## What if you can't attend?

You don't have to send a gift. The [Emily Post Institute](https://emilypost.com/advice/baby-shower-top-etiquette-questions) says that "declining the invitation does not carry with it the weight of needing to send a gift." A warm note with your regrets is enough. You can still send something if you'd like, or wait until the baby is born.

## How much to spend on baby shower prizes (if you're hosting)

Game prizes are usually the host's cost, not something guests pay for. [WebBabyShower](https://webbabyshower.com/guides/baby-shower-prizes-under-10-dollars) suggests keeping each prize **under $10** so the total stays on budget. Plan one prize per game, and add runner-up prizes if you can afford them.

As a hypothetical example: five games with one prize each, at $5 to $10 per prize, would cost about $25 to $50 in total. Bulk deals, sales and small consumables like candles, treats or small gift cards help keep costs down.

## Make gift spending easier all year

Baby showers often come in clusters, and weddings, birthdays and holidays tend to crowd the same months. Setting aside a small amount each month for gifts takes the pressure off any single event.

If you're the one expecting, it may help to [budget for your baby's first year](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-budget-for-a-babys-first-year-without-guessing/).

## The short version

- **Acquaintance or coworker:** around $20 to $30, or $18 to $35 toward an office group gift (US)
- **Close friend:** around $50
- **Close family:** around $100, if your budget allows
- **Baby sprinkle:** usually less than a first shower, often $20 to $50
- **Tight budget:** a group gift, practical essentials or something handmade

Whatever you spend, what the parents-to-be will remember most is that you showed up for them.
