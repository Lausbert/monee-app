Navigating the first few months in your new home in Germany will be exciting but may also be expensive and filled with unfamiliar costs. Rental deposits, insurance, registration fees and new monthly bills may all arrive at once, likely before you've even received your first German payslip. Having a clear budget from day one will help you settle in without money stress.

Here's a step-by-step guide to get you started.

## 1. Start with your take-home pay

Your budget should be built on your net income, not your gross salary. In Germany, income tax and social contributions (health, pension, unemployment and long-term care insurance) are deducted before you're paid. According to the [Federal Ministry of Finance](https://www.bundesfinanzministerium.de/Monatsberichte/Ausgabe/2026/07/Inhalte/Kapitel-2-Analysen/2-3-steuern-im-internationalen-vergleich-2025.html), a single average-wage earner pays around 38.7% of their gross salary in income taxes and social security contributions. If you register as a member of a recognised church, you'll also pay a percentage of your income tax as church tax – the amount will vary depending on your state.

It's best to use an online gross-to-net calculator, or wait for your first payslip, and budget from that figure.

## 2. Get housing costs right

Rent is usually your biggest expense, and German listings use two terms you need to know. *Kaltmiete* (cold rent) is the base rent only. *Warmmiete* (warm rent) adds service charges such as heating, water and building maintenance. Always budget using the warm rent.

Electricity and internet are often billed separately, so check what's included before you sign for these services. You'll also need a security deposit, which is legally capped at three months' cold rent. It's a good idea to set this money aside early, as it's typically due before you move in.

If you're living in Germany part-time or keeping a property in your home country for any other reason, make sure you account for any [overseas payments](https://www.remitly.com/de/de/money-transfer) in your budget.

## 3. List your fixed monthly costs

Next, add any recurring fixed monthly costs to your budget. They may include:

- **Health insurance:** This is mandatory in Germany. If you're employed with public cover, it's deducted from your salary; private or self-employed cover is paid separately.
- **Broadcasting fee:** This is charged per household, whether or not you own a TV.
- **Personal liability insurance:** It's not compulsory, but is widely held and generally inexpensive.
- **Transport:** A monthly public transport pass such as the Deutschlandticket, the nationwide season ticket, can be useful.
- **Phone and internet:** Contracts often run for 24 months, so make sure you compare offers before committing.

## 4. Plan for one-off setup costs

Relocating to Germany comes with some costs you'll only pay once, but they can add up. Budget separately for furniture and kitchen equipment (many German flats are let unfurnished, sometimes even without a fitted kitchen), moving and shipping, temporary accommodation, and translations of official documents.

You'll also need to register your address [within two weeks of moving in](https://verwaltung.bund.de/portal/DE/info-pages/umzug). Registration itself is usually free, but it unlocks your tax ID, which your employer needs to deduct the right amount of tax from your pay. Until then, a cash buffer covering two to three months of expenses can be a helpful safety net – just don't forget to [track any cash payments you make](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-track-cash-spending-when-you-forget-to-log-it/).

## 5. Track, review and adjust

With your income and fixed costs mapped out, what's left covers groceries, eating out, leisure and savings. Track every expense for your first three months, because that's when you'll learn what life in your new home really costs. Then review your budget and adjust your categories as needed.
