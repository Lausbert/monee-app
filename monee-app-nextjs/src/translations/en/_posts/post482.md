To help your teen budget their first paycheck, keep it short and hands-on. Read the pay stub together so they can see where the money went. Then help them split their take-home pay into a few simple buckets, like saving, spending and a goal. Agree on which costs are now theirs to cover, and check in again after a month. Your job is to coach, not to control. A budget your teen builds on their own will teach more than a perfect one you build for them.

This guide walks through each step. The tax and account details apply to the US. If you live elsewhere, check your own country's rules.

## Start before the first paycheck arrives

A little setup makes the first payday much easier.

- **Open an account in their name or a joint account.** The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) recommends helping young workers open a free or low-cost account at a bank or credit union. It notes that having a bank account is linked to [higher levels of financial literacy in young workers](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/blog/more-just-fun-sun-summer-money-matters-young-workers). Teens under 18 usually need an adult co-owner.
- **Ask about how they'll get paid.** The CFPB points out that [direct deposit is often a free and easy option](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/blog/more-just-fun-sun-summer-money-matters-young-workers) when the worker has an affordable account. If the employer pays on a payroll card instead, look up the fees together.
- **Find out the pay schedule.** First paychecks often show up two or more weeks after the first shift. If your teen will have costs before then, like gas or work shoes, plan for them using [a simple plan for the gap before the first paycheck](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-budget-the-gap-before-your-first-paycheck/).

## Read the pay stub together

Many teens are surprised that their paycheck is smaller than hours × hourly rate. Going through the stub line by line turns that surprise into a lesson.

**Gross pay vs. net pay.** Gross pay is what they earned. Net pay, or take-home pay, is what lands in the account after deductions. The budget is built on net pay.

**Common US deductions:**

- **Social Security and Medicare (FICA).** In the US, employees pay 6.2% for Social Security and 1.45% for Medicare, which adds up to [7.65% of wages](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc751). Most teens pay this no matter how little they earn.
- **Federal income tax withholding.** The amount depends on the Form W-4 your teen filled out. Some students qualify to claim "exempt." They can do that only if they had no federal income tax liability last year *and* expect none this year. The exemption covers income tax only, not Social Security or Medicare, and it has to be renewed every year ([IRS, Form W-4](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-w-4)).
- **State and local taxes**, depending on where you live.

**Hypothetical example:** A teen works 20 hours over a two-week pay period at $15 an hour. Gross pay is $300. FICA takes $22.95 (7.65%). If no income tax is withheld, net pay is about $277. State taxes, if any, would lower that a bit more. Showing the real math helps teens see why "$15 an hour" doesn't mean $15 in their pocket.

## Decide who pays for what

Agree on this before the money arrives, or every purchase can turn into a negotiation. Write down which costs are now your teen's, for example:

- Their share of gas or transit
- Their phone bill or part of it
- Fun money: food with friends, games, events
- Clothes beyond the basics you cover

Start small. Handing over one or two real bills gives your teen practice without overwhelming them. You can add more as they get comfortable.

## Split take-home pay into simple buckets

Teens don't need a 20-line budget. Three or four buckets are enough:

1. **Save:** a cushion for surprises, like a cracked phone screen or a car repair.
2. **Goal:** something specific they care about, such as a car, a trip, a laptop or college costs.
3. **Committed costs:** the bills they agreed to cover.
4. **Spend freely:** guilt-free money they can use however they like.

There's no official split. Let your teen choose the percentages and explain why. **Hypothetical example:** using the $277 paycheck above, a teen might pick 20% savings (about $55), 30% for a goal (about $83), $40 for phone and gas, and the rest (about $99) to spend. If the spending bucket is too small, they'll likely ignore the plan, so don't shrink it to zero.

If they like shopping, [a dupe-first shopping budget](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-build-a-dupefirst-shopping-budget-that-saves-money-without-killing-your-style/) can make their spending money go further without feeling like a punishment.

## Make the plan easy to follow

Good intentions fade by the second weekend. A few habits help:

- **Move savings first.** Set up an automatic transfer to a savings account on payday, so saving happens before spending.
- **Use separate places for separate buckets.** A second savings account or labeled sub-accounts make it easy to see each bucket.
- **Track for one month.** An app, a notes file or a spreadsheet all work. The goal is for your teen to see where the money really went.

## Plan for uneven hours

Teen jobs often have shifts that change week to week, especially in retail, food service or seasonal work. If paychecks vary, help your teen budget from a "low" paycheck and send any extra to savings or their goal. For more detail, see this [playbook for budgeting on irregular income](https://monee-app.com/blog/paycheck-budgeting-for-irregular-income-a-practical-playbook/).

## Review after a month, without judgment

Set up one short check-in after the first month. Ask open questions:

- What surprised you?
- Which bucket ran out first?
- Do you want to change the percentages?

Your teen will probably overspend at some point. That's fine. Running short on fun money at 16 is a cheap lesson compared with running short on rent at 23. Before you step in to cover a shortfall, decide whether a rescue will help them or keep them from learning. If you do lend money, treat it like a real loan with a repayment date.

## Talk about a few traps early

- **Early paycheck and cash advance apps.** Some apps let workers get paid before payday, often for a fee or a "tip." Help your teen weigh the cost with [this guide to whether earned wage access apps are worth it](https://monee-app.com/blog/how-to-decide-if-earned-wage-access-early-paycheck-apps-are-worth-it-with-a-costspeed-matrix/).
- **Subscriptions.** Small monthly charges add up quickly on a part-time income. Go through their subscriptions together each month.
- **Buy now, pay later.** Explain that splitting a purchase into payments uses up future paychecks.

## Explain taxes at the end of the year

**Filing a return (US).** For tax year 2026, a single dependent with only earned income generally has to file a federal return if that income is more than $16,100 ([Credit Karma, citing IRS figures](https://www.creditkarma.com/tax/library/tax-filing-basics/filing-requirements-for-dependents/); see also [IRS Publication 501](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p501)). Even below that amount, filing may be the only way to get back federal income tax that was withheld. Other rules apply to investment income and self-employment income, such as babysitting or lawn mowing, so check the IRS rules for your teen's situation.

**A Roth IRA (optional, US).** Teens with earned income can contribute to a Roth IRA, usually through a custodial account. The 2026 IRA contribution limit is [$7,500](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500). Contributions can't be more than the person's taxable compensation for the year ([IRS, Roth IRAs](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/roth-iras)). Some families offer a "match," where the parent puts in money alongside the teen's contribution. It's not required. For many teens, an emergency cushion and a near-term goal matter more right now.

## A quick checklist for payday

- Account open and direct deposit set up
- Pay stub reviewed: gross pay, deductions and net pay
- Agreement on which costs are now your teen's
- Take-home pay split into save, goal, bills and spending
- Automatic savings transfer set up
- One-month review on the calendar

A first paycheck is a chance to teach money skills while the stakes are still small. Explain the numbers, agree on clear rules and give your teen room to make their own choices. Each payday after that will be easier for both of you.
