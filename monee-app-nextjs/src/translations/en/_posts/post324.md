If you were charged more than your quote or estimate, contact the company before you pay. Ask them to explain the difference line by line, and ask them to change the bill to match what you agreed to. Have your original quote ready. Then, if needed, ask for someone with authority to approve billing adjustments. You can open with this:

> "Hi, I'm calling because my bill is higher than the quote I was given. I'd like to go over the difference and ask for the bill to be adjusted to match the original quote."

That's enough to get started. You aren't being difficult. You're asking the company to stick to the price it gave you.

You don't need to accuse anyone. You want the bill reviewed, corrected or clearly explained. Most people freeze because they don't know how to start, so the scripts below give you the words.

## First, check whether you got a quote or an estimate

Your leverage depends on what you were given. The rules differ by country, but in the UK, [Citizens Advice](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/getting-home-improvements-done/problem-with-home-improvements/) explains the difference clearly:

- **A quote** (also called a "quotation" or "fixed estimate") is a promise to do the work for an agreed price. A trader can't charge more without a good reason. Good reasons include you agreeing to pay for extra work, or the quoted price being an obvious mistake. Citizens Advice says traders can't charge more just because their own costs went up after they quoted.
- **An estimate** is the trader's best guess. The final bill can be higher, but if it's a lot more than you expected, you can still dispute it.

In the UK, if your contract didn't fix a price, section 51 of the [Consumer Rights Act 2015](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/section/51) says you only have to pay "a reasonable price for the service, and no more."

In other countries, check your paperwork for words like "fixed price," "estimate," "not to exceed" or "subject to change." Those words tell you how firm the number was.

## Gather your facts (five minutes)

Find the original quote or estimate, any confirmation email, contract, order summary, in-app price screen, screenshots or notes from the conversation. If you track your spending, you can say, "I looked at my spending and noticed this bill is higher than expected." That sounds clear and grounded.

Write down:

- The quoted price or rate: [amount]
- The final bill amount: [amount]
- The date you got the quote: [date]
- The name of the person or company who gave it
- Any fees, charges or rate changes you don't recognize

Now you're ready.

## Phone script

Use this when you want the fastest answer.

> "Hi, my name is [name]. I received a bill for [service/product], and it is higher than the quote I was given. The quote I received on [date] was [amount], but the bill shows [amount]. I'd like to understand why there is a difference and have the bill adjusted if the higher amount was charged in error."

Then pause and let them respond.

If they say, "Let me check," say:

> "Thank you. I have the original quote available if you need the details."

If they say, "Those are standard fees," say:

> "I understand there may be standard fees, but they were not included or explained in the quote I received. Since I made the decision based on that quote, I'm asking for those fees to be waived or the bill adjusted."

If they say, "There's nothing we can do," say:

> "I hear you. Could you please check whether a supervisor or billing specialist has the authority to review this? I'm not asking for a favor. I'm asking for the bill to reflect the quote I was given."

After you ask, stay quiet. Silence is useful, and you don't need to fill it.

## Chat script

Chat gives you a written record.

> "Hi, I need help reviewing a bill that is higher than the quote I received. The quote on [date] was [amount], but my bill shows [amount]. Can you please explain the difference and help adjust the bill to match the original quote?"

If they ask for proof:

> "Yes, I can provide the quote details. It was sent/confirmed on [date], and it listed [amount] for [service/product]."

If they say the charge is valid:

> "I understand that is what the current bill shows. My concern is that I agreed to the service based on the quote I was given. Please review whether the extra charge can be removed, reduced, or escalated."

If the chat agent can't help:

> "Please escalate this to someone who can review billing adjustments. I'd also like a copy of this chat or a reference number for follow-up."

## Email script

Use email if the bill is complicated or you want everything documented.

Subject line options:

- "Request to Review Bill Higher Than Quote"
- "Billing Adjustment Request for [account/order number]"
- "Invoice Does Not Match Original Quote"

Email:

> Hello,  
>   
> I'm writing because my bill for [service/product] is higher than the quote I received.  
>   
> The quote provided on [date] was [amount]. The bill I received shows [amount]. I agreed to move forward based on the original quoted amount, so I'd like the bill reviewed and adjusted to match the quote.  
>   
> Please confirm why the amount changed and whether the extra charge can be waived, reduced, or corrected. I can provide the original quote details if needed.  
>   
> Thank you,  
> [name]

It's polite, clear and firm, and that combination works.

## When the price in an app doesn't match your invoice

Sometimes the rate quote in an app differs from the final invoice. This happens with rides, deliveries, movers, repairs and bookings. The cause is often something the app's terms allow to change after you book, such as wait time, route changes, tolls, extra items, taxes or a price shown "from" a starting amount. Each provider's terms are different, so ask for the exact reason:

> "The app showed me [amount] when I booked on [date], but I was charged [amount]. Can you show me which line items changed after booking and where those were disclosed before I confirmed?"

Screenshot the in-app price and booking confirmation before they disappear from your history. In the US, the FTC's rule on unfair or deceptive fees has required businesses selling **live-event tickets and short-term lodging** (hotels and vacation rentals) to show the total price upfront since [May 12, 2025](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/rule-unfair-or-deceptive-fees-frequently-asked-questions). The rule only covers those two industries. If a hotel or ticket seller added mandatory fees that weren't in the price you were shown, say so.

## If they blame taxes, fees or add-ons

Sometimes the bill is higher because of fees, upgrades, service charges, installation, processing fees or extra items added after the quote.

Say:

> "I understand there may be additional charges in some cases. My concern is that these were not disclosed when I was quoted [amount]. I'm asking for a review because I would not have agreed without knowing the full cost."

If part of the bill is correct and part isn't, be specific:

> "I'm not disputing the full bill. I'm disputing the difference between the quoted amount and the final amount."

That keeps the conversation focused. If the higher bill is for gas or electricity, [what to say when your energy bill suddenly jumps](https://monee-app.com/blog/what-to-say-when-your-energy-bill-suddenly-jumps/) covers meter readings and tariff changes.

## If the first try doesn't work

Don't treat the first "no" as final. Many first-line representatives can only approve so much.

Try this:

> "I understand this may be outside your approval level. Could you please escalate this to a supervisor or billing department?"

If they still refuse:

> "Please send me a written explanation of why the bill cannot be adjusted, including where the additional charges were disclosed before I agreed."

That turns the conversation from opinions into documents.

You can also ask for a partial outcome:

> "If the full adjustment is not possible, what can you offer? Could the fee be waived, the rate reduced, or the plan changed going forward?"

That gives them options without you dropping the issue. If you need breathing room while it's sorted out, here's [what to say when you need more time to pay a bill](https://monee-app.com/blog/what-to-say-when-you-need-more-time-to-pay-a-bill/).

## Formal options if talking doesn't fix it

These depend on where you live and how you paid.

**US: medical bills for uninsured or self-pay patients.** Under the No Surprises Act, you can use the federal patient-provider dispute resolution process if a provider or facility billed you [at least $400 more than its good faith estimate](https://www.cms.gov/medical-bill-rights/help/dispute-a-bill). You must have that estimate in writing, and you must start the dispute within 120 calendar days of the date on your first bill. CMS lists a $25 non-refundable administrative fee. While the dispute is open, the provider can't send the bill to collections or charge late fees. If you have insurance, different rules apply, so start with your insurer.

**US: credit card charges.** Under the Fair Credit Billing Act, a charge for the wrong amount counts as a billing error. The [FTC says](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/using-credit-cards-and-disputing-charges) your written dispute must reach the card issuer within 60 days after the first bill showing the error was sent to you. Send it to the billing-inquiries address, not the payment address. The issuer must acknowledge it within 30 days and resolve it within 90. While it investigates, you can withhold the disputed amount, but you still have to pay the rest of the bill.

**UK: quotes, estimates and card payments.** If a trader won't honour a quote, [Citizens Advice](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/getting-home-improvements-done/problem-with-home-improvements/) has template letters, and its consumer helpline is 0808 223 1133. If you paid by credit card and the cost was over £100 and up to £30,000, [Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/consumer-rights/advice/how-to-avoid-building-work-disputes) notes that your card provider may be jointly liable under Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act.

If the overcharge went onto someone else's card in a shared household, see [what to do when a shared bill hits the wrong card](https://monee-app.com/blog/what-to-do-when-a-shared-bill-hits-the-wrong-card/).

## What "bill raised" means

In UK and business English, "a bill has been raised" usually means an invoice has been issued, not that the price went up. So a message saying "we've raised your bill" may only mean your invoice is ready. Check the amount against your quote before you assume it's been increased.

## Keep your tone steady

You don't need to sound angry to be taken seriously. Calm often works better.

Use phrases like:

- "Help me understand…"
- "I'd like this reviewed…"
- "That was not disclosed in the quote…"
- "I agreed based on the amount provided…"
- "What are my options for adjustment?"

Avoid phrases like:

- "This is a scam."
- "You people always do this."
- "I'm not paying anything."

Even if you feel that way, these lines usually make the conversation harder.

## Final script to keep nearby

If you remember nothing else, use this:

> "I received a bill that is higher than the quote I was given. The quote was [amount] on [date], and the bill is [amount]. I agreed based on the original quote, so I'd like the difference reviewed and the bill adjusted."

That's the whole conversation in one calm paragraph.

You're allowed to ask questions about a bill, and you're allowed to expect clear pricing. The better prepared you are with your numbers, the steadier you'll feel when you speak.
