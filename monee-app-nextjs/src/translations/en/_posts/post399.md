An estimated utility bill can quietly turn into a €400 surprise—and a two-minute meter check is often enough to prevent it. If you have ever opened an annual electricity or gas statement while making dinner and immediately lost your appetite, this is for you.

## The quick version

To reduce the risk of estimated bills:

1. Check your electricity, gas, and water meters once a month.
2. Photograph each meter so you have a dated record.
3. Enter the readings in your provider’s online account.
4. Compare the latest number with the previous month.
5. Check every bill for words such as “estimated” or “calculated.”
6. Contact the provider quickly if the reading is wrong.

Yes, this takes about 10 minutes a month. No, it will not magically lower the price per kilowatt-hour. But it can stop inaccurate estimates from hiding a growing bill until the annual statement arrives.

## Why utility companies estimate your usage

Providers usually estimate consumption when they do not receive a valid meter reading. The estimate may be based on the previous year, the former tenant’s usage, or an average household.

That sounds reasonable until real life gets involved.

Perhaps you had a baby and started running the washing machine constantly. Maybe one parent began working from home. Or your teenager discovered 25-minute showers. Your actual consumption can look very different from last year’s neat little calculation.

The opposite happens too. If you reduced your heating but the provider estimates higher usage, you may pay too much and wait months for the correction.

For context, these examples assume a family of four living in a German city. Depending on the home, energy contract, heating system, and habits, a corrected annual bill could differ from the estimate by roughly €100 to €800 or more.

## Build a meter-check routine you can actually keep

I tried setting a special monthly “finance evening.” It failed because nobody wants to inspect meters after bedtime chaos while surrounded by unfolded laundry.

What worked was attaching the check to something that already happened: the monthly household budget review.

Pick one date, such as the first Sunday of every month. Then:

- Find the meter number on your bill and match it to the physical meter.
- Write down the reading exactly as displayed.
- Take a clear photo showing both the reading and meter number.
- Save it in a phone folder named “Meters 2026.”
- Submit the reading through the provider’s website or app.
- Record the number in your budgeting notes.

If you manage money together, store the information somewhere both adults can access. A shared household in Monee can help you track utility payments without another round of “Did you pay that?” The useful part is finally seeing the bills alongside the rest of your family spending.

## Check for unusual jumps

One reading alone tells you very little. The pattern matters.

Subtract last month’s reading from this month’s reading to calculate your consumption. If your electricity meter increased by 280 kWh one month and normally rises by 180–220 kWh, investigate.

Start with ordinary explanations:

- Colder weather
- School holidays
- More cooking or laundry
- Guests staying over
- Electric heaters
- A new tumble dryer or freezer

If nothing explains the increase, check whether an appliance is malfunctioning or whether the reading was entered incorrectly. A transposed number can create an impressively terrifying bill.

## What to do when a bill is estimated

Look for wording such as “estimated reading,” “calculated consumption,” or the German terms *geschätzt* and *rechnerisch ermittelt*. Then compare the billed meter reading with your current meter.

Photograph the meter and contact the provider. Keep the message simple:

> Hello, my bill dated [date] uses an estimated meter reading of [number]. The actual reading on [date] is [number]. I have attached a photo showing the meter number and reading. Please correct the bill and confirm the updated amount and payment date.

Do not cancel a direct debit without first understanding the consequences. Ask whether the payment will be paused or adjusted while the correction is processed.

## The move-in and move-out checks matter most

On moving day, photograph every meter before the boxes block access. Record the date, address, meter number, and reading. If possible, have the landlord, previous tenant, or handover representative confirm the numbers.

I once relied on a handwritten figure buried in moving paperwork. Finding it three months later was not my finest filing moment. A phone photo would have saved several emails and a lot of unnecessary doubt.
